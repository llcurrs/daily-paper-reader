# Multimodal deep learning from spectra for small-molecule structure identification: enhancing robustness with mixed-condition training

Abstract 

In practical molecular characterization, small-molecule structure identification benefits from complementary spectroscopic evidence, but missing, degraded, or mismatched spectra chal-lenge multimodal models. Herein, we incorporate domain knowledge from spectroscopy and chemistry into mixed-condition training for candidate structure reranking, using a reproducible evaluation protocol and mixture-of-experts (MoE) fusion. The protocol incorporates perturba-tions tailored to each spectroscopic modality and chemically informed spectrum replacements to cover variations in spectral availability, quality, and consistency. A total of 79,462 test samples were evaluated across 30 predefined conditions using simulated spectra from the Multimodal Spectroscopic Dataset (MSSD) for mass spectrometry (MS), infrared (IR) spectroscopy, and 

> 1

H and 13 C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), with up to 128 hard candidate structures per sample. A controlled two-by-two factorial comparison of complete-input training versus mixed-condition training and vanilla concatenation versus MoE fusion, with matched evalua-tion conditions, showed that mixed-condition training provided the main gains in both archi-tectures. For MoE, mean reciprocal rank (MRR), averaged equally across conditions, increased from 0.9203 to 0.9763, a relative increase of 6.08%. Recall at rank 1 (R@1), averaged over the same conditions, increased from 89.50% to 96.36%, an increase of 6.86 percentage points and a relative increase of 7.67%. IR-only and MS/MS-only MRR increased from 0.4337 to 0.9307 and from 0.3711 to 0.8575, reaching 2.15 and 2.31 times their respective baseline values, while complete-input performance remained high. These results support integrating domain knowl-edge into training-condition design to improve robustness, with further gains from MoE under mixed-condition training. 

Keywords: Deep learning, Multimodal fusion, Domain knowledge, Spectroscopy, Molecular identification, Robustness, Mixed-condition training 

1. Introduction 

Small-molecule structure identification relies on spectroscopic measurements and their inter-pretation. Structural confirmation commonly requires several complementary measurements, since each probes a different aspect of the molecule. For example, mass spectrometry (MS) can provide fragmentation patterns that inform substructures and connectivity; infrared (IR) spectroscopy reflects molecular vibrations associated with functional groups and bonding envi-ronments; nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provides chemical shifts and cou-pling information associated with the local environments of the observed nuclei. Structure elucidation therefore requires joint analysis of these complementary spectroscopic observations.

However, joint interpretation of these spectra has traditionally relied on accumulated expertise and time-consuming manual analysis. With recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and the development of multimodal spectroscopic datasets, AI-accelerated structure elucidation from complementary spectroscopic evidence has become possible [1–5]. For example, QM9S, developed by Zou et al., provides calculated IR, Raman, and ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectra for approximately 0.13 mil-lion small organic molecules [1]; the Multimodal Spectroscopic Dataset (MSSD), developed by Alberts et al. at IBM Research, pairs approximately 0.79 million molecular structures with simulated IR, NMR, and MS spectra, a scale more readily achievable through simulation than through fully paired experimental measurements [2]. In the MSSD benchmark, an encoder– decoder transformer was used to combine 1H NMR and 13 C NMR spectra with a molecular-formula prior, achieving 73.38% Top-1 and 89.98% Top-10 structure-prediction accuracy on simulated data. Tan used a QM9S-derived dataset to develop CLAMS, a Transformer-based generative model that combined IR, UV–Vis, and 1H NMR spectra to achieve 45.2% Top-1 and 83.1% Top-15 structure-generation accuracy on held-out simulated data [3]. Trained on a larger collection of simulated spectra for 4 million ZINC compounds, the MultiModalSpec-tralTransformer (MMST), developed by Priessner et al., achieved 72% Top-1 and 80% Top-3 accuracy on a 4,000-compound simulated test subset after heteronuclear single quantum coher-ence (HSQC) matching and candidate ranking [4]. In a retrieval setting, SECS, developed by Mirza et al., aligned spectral and molecular representations through contrastive learning and recovered the correct structure at rank 1 for 98.4% of 1,000 MSSD-derived test molecules by combining IR, 1H NMR, 13 C NMR, and HSQC spectra [5]. These approaches are designed to integrate complementary spectral information for molecular structure elucidation. However, in practice, limited experimental resources and sample constraints may prevent the acquisition of a complete set of spectra for a given sample, while sample preparation and instrument settings can introduce noise and accidental sample mismatches may also occur. There is therefore a need to develop targeted training strategies to improve the robustness of multimodal models to incomplete, noisy, or mismatched spectral inputs, and to systematically evaluate their effects on predictive performance and quantify the resulting robustness gains. Herein, we introduce a mixed-condition training strategy (denoted as mixed training), which integrates spectroscopic and chemical domain knowledge into small-molecule candidate struc-ture reranking. Missing-input conditions are combined with modality-specific signal perturba-tions and spectrum replacements with similar structures to cover variations in spectral avail-ability, quality, and consistency. Defined input perturbations and evaluation settings provide a common basis for a controlled two-by-two factorial comparison of complete-input training (denoted as clean training) versus mixed training, and vanilla concatenation (Concat) versus mixture-of-experts (MoE) fusion. This comparison shows that mixed training strategy pro-vides the main robustness gains in both architectures, particularly with sparse, missing, or mismatched evidence, while high complete-input performance is retained. MoE provides a modest additional ranking benefit under mixed training, suggesting that this designed train-ing strategy may serve as a useful foundation for further gains through fusion architecture refinement. 

2. Methods 

2.1. Candidate structure reranking task 

The task was to rank a finite set of candidate molecular structures using a query’s available spectra (Fig. 1). For each query, the set contained the correct structure and a collection of alternative structures. Spectral encoders and a fusion module produced a query representation, while a molecular encoder represented each candidate (Table S1). A compatibility score then 2determined the candidate order. Model testing was conducted without prior knowledge of the molecular formula. The task assessed discrimination within the supplied candidate set, conditional on inclusion of the correct structure. 2.2. Spectroscopic data and candidate sets 2.2.1. Data sources and splits 

MSSD version 3, comprising 794,386 aligned multimodal records, was used in this study. All spectra were computationally generated [2, 6–9]. Four spectroscopic modalities were represented across five input channels: IR, 1H NMR, 13 C NMR, and liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) acquired in positive- and negative-ion modes (ESI + and ESI -). The two mass-spectral channels were predicted using competitive fragmentation modeling for metabolite identification (CFM-ID) at a collision energy of 40 eV, with protonated and deprotonated precursor ions assigned as [M + H] + and [M − H] −, respec-tively [9]. Following preprocessing and eligibility assessment across all five channels, 794,343 samples were retained and partitioned for the study (Table 1). Records sharing the same molecu-lar connectivity, including stereochemical variants, were assigned to the same dataset split. International Chemical Identifier Keys (InChIKey) were generated from molecular structures represented by canonical SMILES, and molecules were grouped by the first InChIKey block (connectivity block). The resulting groups were assigned to the training, validation, and test sets to achieve an approximate 80:10:10 split. 2.2.2. Candidate set construction 

Negative candidates were drawn from an external pool of approximately 14.5 million dedu-plicated molecular structures constructed from PubChem. Structures were standardized with RDKit version 2026.3.2 to canonical isomeric simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) strings, preserving stereochemical information present in the source structures. For the evaluation, a neutral exact-mass index window was used to obtain alternatives near the mass of the correct structure, with chemically related hard negatives prioritized within that window (Fig. 1, K-candidates). Candidate identities were held fixed across spectral perturba-tions. Detailed in Supporting information (SI), Section S2. 

2.3. Input conditions and spectral perturbations 

A set of 30 predefined input conditions (hereafter termed views) was used to cover spectral availability, signal quality, and cross-modal consistency, simulating the spectroscopic measure-ments in practice (Fig. 3). The 30 views comprised 1 clean (complete input), 8 missing-input, one random-dropout, 15 noisy, and 5 conflict views. The implementation and full parameter set are described in Section S1.1 and Tables S2–S3. In concise term, clean view (Fig. 3A) included all five unaltered input channels. The eight missing-input views (Fig. 3B) represented four single-modality and four leave-one-modality-out configurations, without additional numerical perturbation or source replacement. A binary availability mask determined which channel em-beddings were included during fusion. 2.4. Model architectures and training strategies 

All four models shared the molecular and spectral encoders summarized in Table 2. Candi-date structures were represented by ChemBERTa embeddings and candidate-derived chemical features, while DreaMS encoded both MS/MS channels. Local Transformer encoders were used to processed IR and NMR spectra, due to implementation compatibility and pretrained-weight availability. Naive concatenation combined the ordered channel representations with missing inputs zeroed; MoE adaptively pooled the available representations through selected experts. The fused representation was projected into the candidate embedding space for compatibility scoring. Further details are provided in Section S1 and Figs. S1–S2. The factorial comparison crossed Concat or MoE fusion with clean or mixed-view training. Clean training used complete, unperturbed inputs throughout. Mixed training first sampled complete and missing inputs, then introduced noisy and conflict views while retaining complete and sparse inputs. Both strategies optimized candidate cross-entropy with auxiliary chemical-feature and evidence-consistency objectives; MoE also used load balancing. Each combination had one fitted model, with matched total training steps and global batch size. One checkpoint per run was selected by validation MRR on the clean view and evaluated on the same held-out views and hard candidates. Detailed computational settings are listed in Table S1. 

2.5. Evaluation metrics and statistical analysis 

Mean reciprocal rank (MRR) was the primary ranking metric, supplemented by recall at ranks 1, 5, and 10. Expected calibration error (ECE) measured confidence calibration; areas under the receiver operating characteristic and precision–recall curves (AUROC and AUPRC) measured sample-level conflict detection. Channel localization assessed identification of the replaced channel. Ranking excluded padded candidates and assigned the best position within exact score ties; alternative tie conventions were examined separately (Section S3.5). Overall performance was summarized by assigning equal weight to all 30 views. Training, fusion, and interaction effects used paired comparisons. Percentile 95% confidence intervals came from 1,000 bootstrap resamples of test sample identities, preserving pairing across models and views. They quantify test-sample uncertainty for the fixed checkpoints. Metric definitions 6and statistical procedures are given in Section S3.1. Two illustrative cases were selected post hoc using the criteria in Section S1. 

3. Results and discussion

These changes corresponded to a 6.08% relative increase in MRR and a 6.86 pp increase in R@1, or a 7.67% relative increase in first-rank recovery. Concat showed the same main pattern, reaching an MRR of 0.9738 and R@1 of 95.99% with mixed training. IR-only and MS/MS-only inputs most clearly illustrated the recovery of candidate discrim-ination with limited evidence (Fig. 5C). MoE IR-only MRR increased from 0.4337 to 0.9307, reaching 2.15 times the clean-training value. MS/MS-only MRR increased from 0.3711 to 80.8575, or 2.31 times its baseline. The 1H NMR-only score also rose substantially, from 0.7633 to 0.9626 (Table S5), whereas the MoE gain for 13 C NMR-only input was smaller (+0.0487 MRR). Concat exhibited corresponding improvements, including IR-only MRR from 0.4374 to 0.9131 and MS/MS-only MRR from 0.4034 to 0.8631. These evaluations used the same fitted multimodal models with reduced input. The results suggest that the mixed protocol helped preserve candidate discrimination when other measurements were unavailable, consistent with reduced dependence on a fixed combination of spectra. Random channel dropout extended this observation beyond the fixed input masks, with a MoE training gain of +0.0523 MRR (Fig. 5C). The gain varied with the retained evidence. With both MS/MS channels removed, clean-training performance was already high, and MRR increased from 0.9834 to 0.9862. By comparison, removing 13 C NMR yielded a larger improve-ment, from 0.8977 to 0.9814 (Table S5). This dependence on the retained evidence explains why the family mean in Fig. 5A should be read alongside the selected contrasts in panel C and the complete view-by-model comparison in Table S5. All four models retained high MRR when spectral quality was degraded in a single channel and the remaining channels were available. The noisy-family mean for MoE increased from 0.9820 with clean training to 0.9857 with mixed training (Fig. 5A and B). At the highest perturbation level, 1H NMR degradation produced MRR values of 0.9692 and 0.9830 for the two strategies, respectively; the corresponding values for IR were 0.9787 and 0.9843. These higher absolute scores reflect both the clean-view baseline and the response to degradation. Fig. 5D separates the latter by subtracting each model’s own clean-view MRR. For MoE, the 1H NMR decrease narrowed from 0.0158 to 0.0037, and the IR decrease from 0.0063 to 0.0024. Smaller IR and NMR decreases were also observed for Concat, whereas MS/MS changes were close to zero under both strategies. Jointly, panels B and D support higher noisy-input performance, with reduced degradation for IR and NMR. The complete curves in Fig. S3 and Table S5 include some nonmonotonic responses and describe the specified perturbations with complementary channels present. The near-zero MS/MS changes and larger IR and NMR decreases in Fig. 5D motivate a pos-sible spectroscopic interpretation. In ESI-MS/MS, soft ionization produces precursor ions [14], while subsequent collision-induced dissociation generates fragment populations that depend on molecular structure, precursor-ion type, collision energy, and instrument conditions. CFM-ID models competing fragmentation pathways probabilistically [9]; the numerical perturbations used for evaluation were applied separately. The small MS/MS changes could reflect preserva-tion of useful fragment constraints and compensation by the other channels. IR bands reflect molecular vibrations and bonding environments, while one-dimensional 1H and 13 C NMR chem-ical shifts primarily constrain local nuclear environments [8, 13]. Reliance on these features may make ranking sensitive to spectral displacement or distortion. Here, NMR perturbations in-cluded chemical-shift displacement, whereas IR perturbations altered baseline, noise, intensity, and sampled points without explicitly shifting band positions. This interpretation concerns simulated spectra with complementary channels retained and modality-specific perturbation settings, rather than perturbations matched for equivalent information loss. Mixed training also improved ranking with incorrect-source evidence, where an available spectrum belonged to another molecule rather than being numerically degraded. The conflict-family MRR for MoE rose from 0.9324 to 0.9775, and Concat improved from 0.9298 to 0.9747 (Fig. 5A). The greatest MoE gain among the five replacement views occurred for 13 C NMR, where MRR increased from 0.8039 to 0.9710. Improved ranking was accompanied by better sample-level conflict detection: MoE AUROC increased from 0.4961 to 0.8148, while Concat increased from 0.5002 to 0.8546 (Table 3). The corresponding AUPRC values for MoE-mixed and Concat-mixed were 0.8368 and 0.8801. These outcomes distinguish two benefits: prioritiz-ing the correct candidate despite a mismatch, and detecting that the input set contains one. 9Mean channel-localization accuracy under mixed training was 20.38% for MoE and 20.78% for Concat, close to the 20.00% random-choice level across five channels (Table S6). The combined results support improved candidate prioritization and sample-level mismatch screening. Calibration improved alongside ranking. Mean ECE decreased from 8.13% to 1.57% for MoE and from 7.74% to 1.66% for Concat (Table 3). The largest calibration errors for the clean-trained models occurred with sparse evidence, including IR-only and MS/MS-only in-puts, and were substantially reduced after mixed training (Table S5). Improved ordering and lower calibration error address complementary aspects of candidate prioritization: which struc-tures are ranked highest and how well the reported confidence agrees with correctness. Taken together, the view-level results support a training mixture that covers anticipated changes in evidence availability, quality, and consistency while retaining complete-input examples. 

3.3. Fusion architecture effects and interactions 

The value of MoE depended on the training strategy (Fig. 4C). Under mixed training, MoE exceeded Concat by 0.0024 MRR (95% CI, +0.0020 to +0.0028). Under clean training, its difference from Concat was −0.0010 ( −0.0015 to −0.0006). The resulting interaction was +0.0035 (+0.0029 to +0.0041), indicating that the fusion comparison changed in favor of MoE under the mixed protocol (Table S4). Consistent with the aggregate comparison in Fig. 4A and B, MoE achieved higher MRR than Concat in 29 of the 30 predefined views under mixed training. The largest local gain was +0.0176 for IR-only input; MS/MS-only input was the exception, with a difference of −0.0056 (Table S5). The relative magnitudes of these effects provide a useful design priority. Changing the train-ing strategy improved MRR by 0.0525–0.0559 across the two architectures, whereas changing fusion within mixed training added 0.0024. Concat already combined learned spectral repre-sentations through a trainable projection and could accommodate the missing-input patterns encountered during training. MoE supplied an additional mechanism for conditioning fusion on the available representations and mask. Its additional ranking gain is consistent with a benefit from adaptive fusion once diverse input conditions have been included in training. The com-parison therefore prioritizes the complete mixed protocol, with fusion refinement providing an additional improvement. Within mixed training, MoE achieved higher ranking scores, whereas Concat achieved higher conflict-detection scores. The role of domain knowledge lies in the definition of that training protocol. General strategies such as modality dropout and data augmentation were instantiated through modality-specific signal changes and replacement-source selection based on molecular relationships. Adefined sampling schedule, shared hard candidates, and fixed evaluation views made these choices reproducible and allowed their combined benefit to be quantified. The two-by-two factorial comparison therefore supports the mixed protocol as a complete training strategy. Component-wise ablations against a modality-dropout-only schedule could further resolve the contributions of individual perturbations and training stages. Routing diagnostics were consistent with broader expert use on average after mixed train-ing. The 30-view mean normalized expert-selection entropy increased from 0.7510 to 0.8520, and the mean maximum expert-selection share decreased from 0.4155 to 0.3113 (Table S7). Fig. S4 places these averages in context: broader use under clean, noisy, and conflict inputs coexisted with concentrated routing under several fixed missing-input masks. Missing-family entropy decreased from 0.7147 to 0.6817 alongside improved ranking. These patterns are con-sistent with condition-dependent routing. Expert identifiers are local to each fitted model, and expert-selection frequencies are distinct from spectral-channel weights. Section S3.4 further distinguishes these diagnostics from the load-balancing objective, which also depends on router probabilities. Fragmentation patterns can constrain molecular substructures and frameworks, so the larger MS/MS gain is compatible with reduced ambiguity among the random alternatives. Mass, formula, scaf-fold, and similarity changed jointly, so the comparison quantifies the overall effect of candidate composition. For study design, these results emphasize the joint role of input conditions and candidate difficulty. Complete multimodal evidence improved ranking within hard sets that prioritized 11 chemically related alternatives, while random candidates made each single-modality task easier. Evaluation with hard candidates exposed differences in discrimination that were less apparent under broad random sampling. Accordingly, a modality ordering should be interpreted with its model, training strategy, and candidate construction specified. The strict first-rank comparisons (Section S3.6) and the post hoc MS/MS peak-count analysis (Section S3.7; Fig. S5) provide additional context for this interpretation.

The second case concerns inconsistent molecular sources (Fig. 7D–F). For MSSD sam-ple 9267, the correct structure (C) had formula C 27 H30 N4O2S and was ranked first by both models under clean input. The conflict view replaced only its 13 C NMR spectrum with that of MSSD molecule 173387, selected through the similar-functional-group replacement cate-gory. The replacement source (R) had formula C 24 H27 FN 2O2, differing from that of the correct structure despite selection through a functional-group relationship. The overlaid traces in panel D document the altered carbon-environment evidence, while panel E establishes that R was outside the candidate set. With the other four channels from the correct structure retained, MoE-clean placed the correct structure at rank 24 and PubChem CID 68556177 first. MoE-13 mixed instead ranked the correct structure uniquely first and that competitor at rank 21. The 128 valid candidates were identical across both models and both input views, and none of the correct-structure ranks involved tied scores. Thus, panel F illustrates correct-candidate recovery under a fixed source mismatch. These cases connect the aggregate improvements to two different uses of spectroscopic ev-idence: retaining candidate discrimination when most spectra are unavailable, and preserving the correct structure’s priority when one channel is inconsistent with the others. Selected post hoc using the criteria described in the Methods, they illustrate the consequences of a large ranking improvement for a fixed molecular candidate list. The cases provide chemical context for the population-level gains supported by the paired comparisons and complete view-level results. 

4. Conclusion 

A mixed-condition training protocol incorporating spectroscopic signal characteristics and chemical relationships was developed for multimodal candidate structure reranking. A con-trolled comparison using simulated spectra from 79,462 samples across 30 predefined views showed that the complete mixed training protocol provided the main robustness gains. The 30-view mean MRR increased by 6.08% for MoE and 5.69% for Concat relative to clean training. For MoE, R@1 increased by 6.86 percentage points. The largest improvements occurred with incomplete or mismatched evidence, while high clean-view performance was retained. MoE provided a smaller additional MRR gain of 0.0024 under mixed training. Calibration and sample-level conflict detection also improved with mixed training. The comparison of hard and random candidate sets further showed that single-modality reranking performance depended on candidate composition. Together, the training design and fixed-candidate evaluation provide a reproducible basis for robust prioritization of molecular structures from complementary spec-tra, conditional on inclusion of the correct structure in the candidate set. For future studies, developing large-scale datasets of experimentally measured multimodal spectra and improv-ing the localization of inconsistent channels are priorities for advancing this approach toward practical application.
