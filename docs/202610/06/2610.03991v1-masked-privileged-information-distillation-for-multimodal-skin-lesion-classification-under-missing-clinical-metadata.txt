# Masked Privileged-Information Distillation for Multimodal Skin Lesion Classification Under Missing Clinical Metadata 

Anirban Barua, Md Mahir Abrar Khan, Ayman Iktidar, Md. Sajjatul Islam 

Department of Computer Science and Engineering Chittagong Independent University (CIU), Chattogram, Bangladesh 

{anirban barua, mahir abrar khan, ayman iktidar, sajjatulislam }@ciu.edu.bd 

Abstract —Multimodal skin lesion classification combines clini-cal images with patient metadata to improve diagnostic accuracy. However, complete metadata available during training may be only partially accessible at deployment, and resource-constrained settings additionally require computational efficiency. We address these challenges with a privileged-information distillation frame-work in which a multimodal teacher trained on complete meta-data supervises a 9.2 × smaller student trained with randomly masked clinical fields. Clinical fields are masked as whole groups at a per-sample rate drawn from U(0,1), so one training run covers the full metadata availability range. Fusion is residual, with metadata added as a gated correction to an unconditional image base. On the PAD-UFES-20 dataset, distillation under masked training improves balanced accuracy over cross-entropy training at every availability level, by an average of +4.7 points versus +1.8 points without masking. The masked student loses only 7.8 balanced-accuracy points as metadata decreases from complete to absent, compared with 36.6 points for the same student trained on complete metadata, highlighting the role of masked training in graceful degradation beyond distillation alone. Grad-CAM visualizations further show that the masked student’s attention generally remains lesion-centered as metadata is withdrawn. The resulting compact model targets point-of-care settings, where clinical metadata is often incomplete. 

Keywords —Multimodal learning, Skin lesion classification, Knowledge distillation, Privileged information, Incomplete meta-data, Metadata masking 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Skin cancer is one of the most commonly diagnosed cancers worldwide, with over 337,000 new melanoma cases and over 1.1 million non-melanoma cases globally [1], emphasizing the need for efficient classification methods to facilitate early diagnosis. Yet, the number of lesions requiring assessment far exceeds what specialist services can evaluate directly, especially in regions where patients are first seen by general practitioners or community health workers rather than derma-tologists [2]. Automated classification from clinical images offers a way to extend triage capacity into these settings. However, these settings often have limited infrastructure and thus require models to run on commodity hardware or mobile devices at the point of care. This creates a fundamental constraint on model size and inference cost, regardless of any accuracy gains that might be obtained by using larger models. Multimodal classifiers improve on image-only models by incorporating patient metadata, such as age, lesion location, diameter, and other clinically relevant information that pro-vides diagnostic cues beyond visual appearance. This reflects clinical practice, where physicians combine visual examination with patient history rather than relying on images alone. In deployment, however, not all metadata used to develop a machine learning model is present. Patients may be unable to provide details such as family history, intake forms may be incomplete, and certain fields may be omitted due to time constraints. Thus, a model trained and optimized under the assumption of full metadata may perform poorly when deployed in these variable conditions. This, together with the computational constraint discussed above, motivates the development of a single lightweight model that can robustly handle varying levels of metadata availability without the need for retraining. Robustness to missing metadata and model compactness have largely been treated as separate concerns in multimodal skin lesion classification. Rather than treating the develop-ment to deployment gap as a limitation, we exploit complete training-time metadata as privileged information to guide a student model designed to operate across varying levels of metadata availability at inference. Using the PAD-UFES-20 dataset [3], this work trains a multimodal ConvNeXt-Base teacher on complete metadata and distills it into a smaller and faster ConvNeXt-Pico student that sees only a randomly masked subset of clinical fields, enabling it to achieve both a small footprint and tolerance for incomplete records. Realistic missingness is simulated by randomly varying the metadata availability rate from 0% to 100%, allowing a single training procedure to handle the full range of metadata availabil-ity. Fusion is residual, so absent metadata can only fail to contribute, never suppress the image signal. The result is a single compact model that degrades gracefully as metadata is withheld, rather than collapsing as conventional multimodal classifiers do under the same conditions. Our contributions are:  

> •

We design a distillation framework that treats a teacher’s complete metadata during training as privileged informa-tion for the student, masking clinical fields at a random availability rate r ∼ U (0 , 1) so that a single model spans the full range of metadata availability without retraining. It uses residual gated fusion, where the image provides arXiv:2610.03991v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 the base representation and metadata contributes a gated correction.  

> •

We show that masked training combined with distilla-tion drives robustness: masked training yields far more graceful degradation than unmasked training as metadata is withdrawn, and distillation adds a substantially larger accuracy gain under masking than without it.  

> •

We deliver a distilled ConvNeXt-Pico student: 9.2×

smaller and 6.2× faster on CPU than the teacher, and more robust than the teacher at low metadata availability. II. R ELATED WORK 

Prior work on multimodal skin lesion classification on PAD-UFES-20 used various strategies for fusing clinical images with patient metadata. One of the earliest works, MetaBlock [4] introduced an LSTM-gate-inspired attention module that scales and suppresses CNN feature maps using one-hot-encoded metadata, yielding a gain of at least 8.2 per-centage points in balanced accuracy (BACC) over image-only baselines. It was validated across five CNN backbones, namely EfficientNet-B4, DenseNet-121, MobileNet-V2, ResNet-50, and VGG-13. MetaBlock-SE [5] replaced one-hot encoding with SBERT sentence embeddings of free-text metadata, reaching 70.2% BACC on PAD-UFES-20 while remaining robust across sim-ulated missingness rates of 0–70%, though performance still fell below image-only baselines beyond 50% dropout. It is the only prior work to address metadata missingness directly, but does so by training and evaluating a separate model at each fixed availability rate, rather than a single model spanning the full continuum at deployment. AtnMetaBlock [6] applied Lasso-filtered clinical vectors within a cross-attention module, reporting a maximum 10.4-point BACC gain over baselines and reaching 78.5% BACC with a VGG-13 backbone, while MiSC [7] adopted a late-fusion design concatenating the class probabilities of sepa-rately trained image and metadata classifiers, transferring rea-sonably to HAM10000 and ISIC-2019. Both assume complete metadata at inference and do not report patient-level splitting. DualRefNet [8] applied a dual-stage refinement strategy: an auxiliary super-resolution task for the visual encoder and class-frequency-based weight regularization of the final classifier layers, achieving strong performance. As with the methods above, it requires metadata at test time and does not re-port patient-level splitting, leaving open the risk of patient leakage between training and test partitions. More recent studies focus on efficiency and reliability: TriMNet [9] re-placed attention-based metadata encoders with a Mamba-based state-space encoder, reaching 82.7% classification accuracy at near-linear complexity, and DermaCalibra [10] combined Bayesian uncertainty estimation with dynamic feature mod-ulation and integrated-gradient attribution, using a 23.6M-parameter model. DermaCalibra addresses missing fields only through simple imputation, without a missingness ablation to validate its claimed graceful degradation. None of the methods discussed above treat missing meta-data as more than a preprocessing or robustness concern; missingness is handled by architectural choice, but never formalized within a principled teacher-student framework. Knowledge distillation (KD) [11] transfers the soft output distribution of a large teacher network into a smaller stu-dent, improving the student’s generalization beyond what its capacity alone would allow. Independently, the learning-using-privileged-information (LUPI) paradigm considers a teacher with access to additional per-instance information available only at training time. Lopez-Paz et al. [12] unified these two views as generalized distillation, showing that KD and LUPI are special cases of a single teacher-student framework in which a teacher trained on privileged features x∗ transfers knowledge to a student restricted to the original input x.This formulation applies directly to clinical metadata, which is naturally privileged: available during model development but often missing or unreliable at deployment. We build on generalized distillation by treating clinical metadata as privileged information across the full range of availability, rather than assuming it is available at a fixed rate. To our knowledge, no prior work on this task trains a single distilled student that covers the full range of deployment-time metadata availability without retraining. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Dataset 

Our method is evaluated on PAD-UFES-20, a dataset of 2298 smartphone-acquired clinical images containing six di-agnostic classes: actinic keratosis (ACK), basal cell carci-noma (BCC), melanoma (MEL), nevus (NEV), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and seborrheic keratosis (SEK). Each image is matched with a clinical questionnaire of 21 fields, including age, anatomical region, lesion diameter, symptom indicators, and personal and family history. The dataset is very imbalanced, with the two most frequent classes accounting for the majority of images (Table I). This imbalance is addressed via class-weighted training and balanced-accuracy evaluation throughout. A stratified 70/15/15 patient-grouped train/validation/test split is used, based on each patient’s modal diagnosis, ensuring that all images from a single patient are entirely contained within a single partition, avoiding data leakage and providing a more difficult and realistic evaluation than the image-level protocols often used in prior work on this dataset.

B. Preprocessing 

All images are resized to 256 ×256, cropped to 224 ×224, and normalised using ImageNet statistics. Training images are augmented with random horizontal/vertical flips, rotations up to 15°, and colour jitter, while validation and test images receive no augmentation. The 21 clinical fields comprise three continuous attributes (age and two lesion diameters) and 18 categorical attributes. Continuous fields are standardized to zero mean and unit variance using training-split statistics only. Categorical fields are one-hot encoded into contiguous field-specific blocks, producing an 81-dimensional metadata vector. Following the dataset’s encoding convention, unanswered en-tries are dropped rather than represented as explicit categories, resulting in all-zero blocks. Our masking scheme reproduces this pattern, making masked categorical fields indistinguish-able from genuinely unanswered ones. 

C. Architecture 

Teacher and student share a similar architecture, differing only in image backbone: a ConvNeXt-Base [13] encoder (CLIP-LAION2B pretrained) for the teacher and a roughly nine-times-smaller, ConvNeXt-Pico encoder for the student. An input image is encoded to fimg by the backbone, and the 81 -dimensional metadata vector is encoded by a three-layer MLP (81 → 512 → 256 → 256 , with batch normalisation, ReLU, and dropout p=0 .1 after each hidden layer) to fmeta .

Residual Gated Fusion: The two modalities are combined so that the image is an unconditional base and metadata enters only as a gated correction. Both features are first projected to a common 512 -dimensional space, himg = Wimg fimg and hmeta =

Wmeta fmeta . A gate g is then computed from their concatenation by a two-layer MLP and used to scale the metadata projection, which is added to the image projection (Eqs. 1–2): 

g = σ(W2 ϕ(W1[himg ; hmeta ])) ∈ R512 (1) 

hfused = himg + g ⊙ hmeta ∈ R512 (2) where ϕ is ReLU, σ the logistic sigmoid, and ⊙ the element-wise product. The gate can reach two extremes: g = 0

yields a purely image-based fused representation, while g = 1

admits the full metadata contribution. The fused vector hfused 

is classified by a two-layer head ( 512 → 256 → 6, with dropout 

p=0 .3 before the first layer, ReLU, and dropout p=0 .2 before the second) into the six diagnostic classes. Because himg is never scaled by the gate, the metadata branch can only add to the fused representation. Under missing metadata the gated correction may become uninformative, but the image representation reaches the classifier intact. We also compare against a concatenation baseline that projects 

[himg ; hmeta ] jointly through a linear layer followed by ReLU, weighting both modalities unconditionally.

E. Privileged-Information Distillation 

The teacher is trained once on complete metadata and then kept frozen. During distillation, it always receives the unmasked record and produces soft targets, while the student sees only the masked metadata. This difference in what the two models can access is what enables privileged information: the teacher can use information that is unavailable to the student, helping the student learn to make reliable predictions even when some metadata is missing. For a training image with label y, let zt and zs be the teacher and student logits, the teacher computed from complete metadata and the student from the masked vector ˜m. The student is trained with a convex combination of a supervised term and a distillation term (Eqs. 5–6):

Class weights are assigned inversely to their frequency in the training set and normalised so that they sum to the number of classes. This gives rarer classes, such as melanoma, greater influence during training. The same weighting scheme is used across all arms, ensuring that it does not affect the comparison between them. We train two arms: the distilled arm uses α = 0 .5, learning from both the labels and the teacher’s soft targets, while the 

cross-entropy arm uses α = 0 , learning from the labels alone under the same masking. The mask of equation (4) is seeded only by epoch, batch index, and batch size, independent of the run seed and of which model is training, so at a fixed seed the two arms receive identical masks every batch. Because the masks are paired across arms and every other factor is held fixed, the difference between the two isolates the effect of privileged supervision. To determine whether robustness comes from masked train-ing itself or from distillation, we additionally train an un-masked variant of each arm, each receiving complete metadata throughout training ( r = 1 ). Evaluation still sweeps the full availability range for every model. Both arms are checkpointed on a mixed-availability validation score, the mean balanced accuracy over r ∈ { 0, 0.5, 1}, rather than on full-metadata validation. Selecting on full metadata would favour check-points that rely heavily on complete records, which is the opposite of our target deployment setting. Instead, the mixed criterion rewards checkpoints that perform well across the full availability range. IV. R ESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

A. Experimental Settings 

All models are trained on a dual-NVIDIA T4 GPU setup. Both teacher and students use the AdamW optimiser with a base learning rate of 10 −4, weight decay 10 −4, and a batch size of 32 , with the pretrained backbone frozen for the first five epochs and a lower learning rate applied to backbone parameters thereafter. The learning rate follows a cosine schedule after unfreezing. Models are trained for up to 40 epochs, with early stopping on the validation criterion. Distillation uses temperature T = 2 and balance α = 0 .5.Each configuration is run over five seeds; we report mean and standard deviation across them. Balanced accuracy (BACC) is our primary metric throughout, given the dataset’s class imbalance.

To confirm that the robustness comes from masked training and not from distillation alone, we train both arms without masking, on complete metadata, and evaluate them along the same sweep levels. At high availability the unmasked models match or slightly exceed their masked counterparts, as expected when the test-time input resembles training. Also, the gap between KD and CE arms is much lower with an average of only 1.8 points. As availability falls they collapse: unmasked KD drops from 0.801 at full metadata to 0.435 at zero, a 36 .6-point span, against a 7.8-point span for the masked student. At zero availability the masked student outperforms the unmasked one by 27 .1 points. Masked training, not distillation, is therefore what produces graceful degradation; distillation improves accuracy on top of it.

E. Qualitative Analysis 

We compare Grad-CAM [14] attributions for the masked and unmasked KD students on the same lesions across the availability sweep to understand why masked training degrades more gracefully than unmasked training. This pattern is com-mon and consistent across training seeds: averaged over five seeds, 96 .6 ± 9.1 (∼28% of test set) lesions are classified correctly by the masked student at every availability level but misclassified by the unmasked student at zero. Fig. 3 shows three representative cases. The masked student’s attribution re-mains lesion-centered throughout the sweep, at times growing sharper as metadata is withdrawn, consistent with the model compensating for missing metadata by relying more heavily on visual evidence. In contrast, the unmasked student’s attribution increasingly shifts toward features unrelated to the lesion as metadata becomes less available, including background hair and a surgical marker spot. These shifts occur alongside prediction errors, consistent with the performance collapse for unmasked training.

V. C ONCLUSION & F UTURE WORK 

We addressed the mismatch between complete metadata available during training and partial metadata available at de-ployment, together with the computational constraints of real world skin lesion diagnosis, via a privileged-information dis-tillation framework in which a ConvNeXt-Base teacher trained on complete metadata supervises a compact ConvNeXt-Pico student trained under randomly masked metadata, fused through a residual gated formulation. On PAD-UFES-20, masked training was the main factor enabling graceful degra-dation as metadata became unavailable, while distillation pro-vided an additional performance benefit. In contrast, the same student architecture trained without masking was substantially less robust to missing metadata. Despite its smaller size and lower computational cost, the resulting student remained com-petitive with the teacher and performed better when metadata was withheld. Grad-CAM analysis further indicated that the masked student’s attention remained more consistently centred on the lesion as metadata became unavailable, supporting the observed robustness. Future work includes external val-idation on independent clinical datasets and exploration of more efficient architectures for resource-constrained clinical deployment. REFERENCES [1] H. Sung, A. M.
