Abstract
Emergency chest X-ray (CXR) triage has a structural modality gap: reports arrive after triage decisions, yet multimodal
foundation models require image-text inputs. We present Variational Risk Minimization (VRM), a distillation framework
that treats LVLM-generated report variants as Monte Carlo samples of latent clinical interpretations. Rather than
distilling from a single teacher target, VRM learns from a variationally marginalized teacher distribution, enabling
uncertainty-aware supervision under missing-modality constraints. Under matched encoder families, VRM outperforms
direct fine-tuning baselines and improves calibration with strong recovery from hallucinated supervision. Marginalized
supervision reduces report-selection instability. In our compact edge-student instantiation, a confidence-gated cascade
reaches AUC 0.941 at 103ms average latency with 20.3% cloud escalation, yielding an explicit reliability-latency
operating point for cloud-edge clinical workflows.
Keywords: Knowledge Distillation, Medical Imaging, Foundation Models, Edge Computing, Connected Health, Chest
X-ray, Triage
1. Introduction
In emergency departments, chest X-ray (CXR) triage
must prioritize patients before radiology reports are writ-
ten. This creates a fundamental mismatch: multimodal
foundation models trained with image–text pairs cannot
access reports at the time triage decisions are needed. We
term this systematic gap structural modality censorship—
a train-test mismatch that arises not from random data loss
but from the clinical workflow itself.
Multimodal models such as Med-SigLIP (Seller-
gren et al., 2025) and BiomedCLIP (Zhang et al., 2023)
achieve strong performance when both image and text are
available, but deploying them in practice poses a double
bind.
Cloud-hosted inference offers model capacity
but introduces latency, connectivity dependence, and
data-governance risk (HIPAA, GDPR). Edge deployment
on clinical workstations preserves privacy and speed but
cannot run large models or compensate for the missing
text modality.

VRM treats the absent report as a latent clinical inter-

2. Related Work
2.1. Structural Modality Censorship in Medical AI
Most medical foundation models are trained and
evaluated under modality availability assumptions that
do not hold at emergency triage time.
Earlier CXR
CNN pipelines improved diagnosis through task-specific
preprocessing such as lung segmentation and rib sup-
pression (Yang et al., 2022). Vision-language pretraining
systems such as BiomedCLIP (Zhang et al., 2023),
CheXzero (Tiu et al., 2022), and Med-SigLIP (Sellergren
et al., 2025) demonstrate strong cross-modal reasoning
when image and text are jointly available. Instruction-
following LVLMs (e.g., LLaVA-Med (Li et al., 2023a),
Med-Gemma (Sellergren et al., 2025), and Med-PaLM
M (Tu et al., 2024)) further improve report-level clinical
generation quality. However, in real triage workflows,
radiology reports arrive after the decision point, creating
a systematic train-test mismatch rather than random
missingness. This motivates treating missing text as a
latent variable problem instead of a standard multimodal
classification problem.
2.2. Distillation Under Missing-Modality Constraints
Classical knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015)
and common extensions (e.g., FitNets, Attention Transfer,
CRD) are primarily designed for settings where teacher
and student observe compatible evidence at supervision
time. Existing missing-modality approaches often rely
on feature alignment, modality dropout, or determinis-
tic imputation, which can under-represent uncertainty in
report-conditioned supervision. VRM differs in target con-
struction: rather than matching a single report-conditioned
teacher output, it distills from a variationally marginalized
teacher distribution over LVLM-sampled reports. This
shifts the central question from “which model has higher
AUC” to “which supervisory target is more stable and in-
formative when one modality is structurally unavailable.”
2.3. Uncertainty, Hallucination, and Reliability-Centered
Deployment
Recent work on semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023)
suggests that hallucinated generations can be interpreted
as prior sampling under uncertainty. In connected-health
deployment, this perspective is especially relevant: the
practical risk is not only average discrimination but pre-
diction volatility, calibration drift, and unsafe overconfi-
dence under ambiguous evidence. Edge-focused model
families (e.g., TinyML (Banbury et al., 2020), Mobile-
ViT (Mehta and Rastegari, 2021), and efficient medical
encoders such as CXR Foundation (Kiraly et al., 2024)
or DINOv2 variants (Oquab et al., 2023; Zedda et al.,
2025; Scholz et al., 2025)) address compute constraints,
but do not by themselves solve supervision reliability un-
der structural modality censorship. Our work positions
marginalization as a reliability mechanism and couples it
with confidence-gated escalation, yielding a deployment-
oriented framework with explicit uncertainty handling.
3. Methods
In this section, we formalize the Variational Risk Min-
imization (VRM) framework. VRM models the missing
report modality as a latent clinical interpretation and esti-
mates the corresponding predictive risk by Monte Carlo
marginalization under the LVLM proposal distribution
q(t|x).
3.1. Problem Formulation
Let D = {(xi, ri, yi)}N
i=1 denote a training set where
xi ∈X is a chest X-ray image, ri ∈R is the corresponding
radiology report, and yi ∈{0, 1} represents the clinical ur-
gency label. At training time, all modalities are available.
However, at inference time (the triage stage), the model
must predict y based solely on x, as reports are generated
post-triage.
Our goal is to learn a student model S : X →[0, 1]2
that minimizes expected risk by approximating the
marginalized predictive distribution induced by a heavy
multimodal teacher T : X × R →[0, 1]2.
3.2. Latent Query Alignment (LQA)
To effectively fuse the asynchronous modalities (im-
age x and text reports r), we propose Latent Query Align-
ment (LQA) (Fig. 2), inspired by the Q-Former archi-
tecture (Li et al., 2023b). LQA utilizes a set of learn-
able latent queries Q = {q1, ..., qM} to bridge the modality
gap. These queries interact with text and image features
through stacked transformer blocks. Following a sequen-
tial interaction pattern, each block first contextualizes
queries with text, then grounds them to image evidence:
Q′ = SelfAttn(Q, Ktext),
(1)
Qout = CrossAttn(Q′, Vimg).
(2)
This residual transformer-style composition is repeated
across layers, enabling non-trivial cross-modal interaction
beyond simple linear fusion and helping the fused repre-
sentation fT retain clinically relevant visual signals while
suppressing background noise.

3.6. Confidence-Gated Cascade Triage
For deployment, we evaluate a two-stage cascade
strategy rather than a strict single-model replace-
ment.
The edge student first predicts urgency score
s(x) = maxy S (y|x) and predictive entropy H(x) =
−P
y S (y|x) log S (y|x). A case is accepted at the edge only
if confidence is high and uncertainty is low:
s(x) ≥τs and H(x) ≤τH.
(9)
Otherwise, the case is escalated to the cloud teacher for
secondary confirmation. To ensure privacy and HIPAA
compliance, escalated cases are either de-identified at the
edge or routed to the hospital’s secure private cloud.
Let α denote the escalation rate, Ls the edge latency,
and Lt the cloud latency. The expected end-to-end latency
is:
E[L] = (1 −α)Ls + αLt.
(10)
This design preserves fast throughput for easy cases while
allocating high-capacity multimodal reasoning to ambigu-
ous cases.

200 cases from the MIMIC-CXR test set). For each such

4.9. Sensitivity to LVLM Sampler Choice
A key concern is whether VRM’s gains depend
critically on the choice of LVLM used for Monte
Carlo report generation. To evaluate this, we replace
MedGemma-27B with three alternative open-source
LVLMs of comparable scale—InternVL2-26B (Chen
et al., 2024b), Qwen2.5-VL-32B (Wang et al., 2024),
and HuatuoGPT-Vision-34B (Chen et al., 2024a)—and
retrain the full VRM pipeline under identical settings
(K = 5, same prompt template, same training hyper-
parameters). These models span 26B–34B parameters,
closely matching MedGemma-27B’s scale, and cover
both medical-specialized (HuatuoGPT-Vision, trained
on PubMedVision) and general-purpose (InternVL2,
Qwen2.5-VL) pretraining, thereby disentangling the
effects of domain specialization from sampler identity.
Table 13 summarizes the results. A strong domain
effect is evident: the two medical-specialized samplers
(MedGemma-27B: 0.842, HuatuoGPT-Vision-34B: 0.839)
substantially outperform the two general-purpose sam-
plers (Qwen2.5-VL-32B: 0.786, InternVL2-26B: 0.779),
producing a mean medical–general gap of ∼5.8% AUC.
This is consistent with the direct classification results in
Table 4, where the same general-purpose models achieve
only 0.36–0.44 AUC under zero/few-shot prompting,
indicating limited radiographic comprehension. General-
purpose LVLMs generate reports with low clinical
fidelity—marked by generic descriptions, missed findings,
and frequent hallucinations—which propagate through
the teacher pipeline as noisy supervision targets. The
BERTScore gap corroborates this: medical samplers align
substantially better with reference reports (0.722–0.731)
than general-purpose samplers (0.571–0.603).
Critically, VRM with general-purpose samplers still
outperforms Standard KD (0.748) by a meaningful mar-
gin (+3.1–3.8%), confirming that even noisy reports con-
tribute partial cross-modal signal through marginalization.
However, these configurations fall well below all direct
FT baselines (DINOv2: 0.821, EVA-X: 0.828), indicating
that report quality is a genuine bottleneck rather than an
interchangeable component. The VRM framework am-
plifies the quality of its input reports; it does not replace
the need for clinically competent generation. This finding
validates the choice of MedGemma-27B as the default
sampler and suggests that future improvements in medical
LVLM quality would directly translate to stronger VRM
students.

5. Discussion
Our findings support a reliability-centered view of
distillation under structural modality censorship. VRM’s
primary contribution is a more stable supervision process:
by distilling from a variationally marginalized teacher
distribution, the student model becomes less sensitive to
stochastic report variations and aligns better with clinical
uncertainty.
Mechanism-Level Effectiveness. Ablation on sample size
K, individual-vs-ensemble analysis, and bootstrap stabil-
ity tests show that marginalization improves both super-
vision quality and deployment stability. VRM effectively
recovers dispersed cross-modal signals, reducing report-
selection variance.
Gains Beyond Discrimination. While VRM adds a
marginal +1.4% AUC over the strongest direct fine-tuning
(FT) baseline, its value is multi-dimensional. We observed
improvements in calibration, deployment stability, and
robustness to hallucinated supervision. VRM acts as a
risk hedge against the ’report lottery’ effect. By marginal-
izing interpretations, we ensure cloud-level reasoning is
distilled with low variance, neutralizing stochastic biases
from single-report generations.
High-quality LVLM
samplers are essential for VRM’s peak performance,
but even general-purpose samplers confirm the value of
marginalization. Crucially, VRM’s calibrated confidence
scores enable a cloud-edge cascade routing that achieves
an AUC of 0.941 at a fraction of full-cloud inference
latency.
Reliability, Safety, and Routing. In emergency triage,
asymmetric risks emphasize the need for robust calibra-
tion and balanced sensitivity-specificity trade-offs. VRM
outperforms direct FT and standard KD under the same
90% sensitivity constraint. Furthermore, confidence-gated
routing allows triage systems to handle routine cases
edge-locally, selectively escalating ambiguous cases to
the cloud while preserving low overall latency.
Positioning Relative to Baselines. Deploying massive,
open-source LVLMs directly is impractical for fast triage.
Edge devices struggle with memory and latency con-
straints, while centralized cloud models face HIPAA pri-
vacy burdens and transmission delays. VRM efficiently
bridges this gap, operating locally with deterministic edge
compute while successfully distilling cloud-level reason-
ing.
Limitations and Future Work. The student model’s ceil-
ing is ultimately bound by teacher knowledge and LVLM
generation quality. The remaining performance gap stems
from the student’s lack of dense clinical context (e.g.,
history) available in text reports. This reflects an inher-
ent information ceiling under structural modality censor-
ship rather than a capacity limitation. Image-only infer-
ence still struggles with context-dependent findings, and
11


marginalization cannot eliminate consistent hallucinatory
biases. Thus, high-uncertainty review mechanisms remain
critical. Future directions include adaptive sampling to
reduce computation and prospective multi-site evaluation
to ensure demographic robustness.
6. Conclusion
We introduce Variational Risk Minimization (VRM),
a distillation framework for structural modality censor-
ship in emergency imaging. The central contribution is
an uncertainty-aware supervision mechanism: missing
reports are modeled as latent clinical interpretations and
distilled through Monte Carlo marginalization rather
than single-report targets.
Empirically, our evidence
supports method effectiveness along four axes:
(1)
encoder-transfer advantage—VRM surpasses direct FT
baselines from the same encoder families, with statistical
significance confirmed by paired DeLong tests (p < 0.01);
(2) supervision quality and stability—performance
improves with K, and report-selection variance is sharply
reduced; (3) LVLM sensitivity—medical-specialized
samplers (26B–34B) are essential for top performance,
while general-purpose samplers still exceed Standard
KD, confirming report quality as a meaningful but
non-gating factor; and (4) reliability and deployment
viability—better calibration, hallucination recovery, and
in our compact edge-student instantiation, 75MB at 14ms
edge latency, plus confidence-gated escalation for high-
uncertainty cases. As contextual performance reference,
VRM remains robust across five datasets (OOD average
AUC 0.806), while cascade routing reaches AUC 0.941 at
103ms average latency with 20.3% cloud escalation.

Broader Impact
VRM is designed to augment, not replace, radiolo-
gist decision-making, serving as a prioritization tool to
surface potentially urgent cases for faster expert review.
While the model demonstrates strong calibration and gen-
eralization, clinical deployment must be accompanied by
appropriate regulatory clearance (e.g., FDA 510(k) or De

and ongoing performance monitoring across demograph-

representation across age, sex, race, and geographic origin
to ensure that VRM does not exacerbate existing health-
care disparities. Nonetheless, we believe that enabling
expert-level triage on consumer-grade hardware has the
potential to meaningfully improve patient outcomes in
resource-limited settings, where timely access to special-
ist radiology interpretation is often the primary bottleneck
in emergency care. Ultimately, our approach shifts the fo-
cus from simple accuracy-seeking to a reliability-centered
paradigm. VRM demonstrates that latent reasoning can
be distilled into edge devices in a high-robustness, low-
variance manner, providing a principled safeguard for
AI-assisted triage in high-stakes clinical workflows.
