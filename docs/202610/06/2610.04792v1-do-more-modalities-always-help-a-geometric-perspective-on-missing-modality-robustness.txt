## Abstract 

Missing modality remains a longstanding challenge in multimodal learning. Ex-isting methods typically address this issue through modality recovery or adaptive strategies. However, they overlook models’ internal cross-modal dependencies formed during multimodal training, which later impair robustness. We system-atically characterize a counterintuitive deployment-time failure mode: models trained on full modalities can underperform unimodal models when one modality is missing at inference time. This pattern appears across diverse architectures, such as fusion models, CLIP-style two-tower models, and vision-language models. We show that such degradation is closely associated with learned cross-modal dependencies in the principal parameter subspaces. Multimodal training induces structured rotations of these subspaces, particularly in cross-modal interaction layers. These rotations are associated with reduced task-aligned margins and larger task-aware representation harm under missing-modality inputs. We pro-pose Geodesic Unlearning (GU) , a lightweight parameter-editing method that leverages Grassmannian subspace geometry for structured subspace correction to improve missing-modality robustness. It rotates the principal input subspace toward a unimodal reference along a geodesic path. We prove that this correction minimizes the distance to the reference within a fixed subspace-distance budget. Experiments across architectures and datasets show that GU improves performance under missing-modality inference while preserving full-modality accuracy, out-performing strong missing-modality robustness baselines. These findings support a geometric view of deployment-time missing-modality degradation and suggest localized subspace editing as a practical route for robustness correction. 

## 1 Introduction 

Multimodal learning has achieved strong performance across language, vision, and healthcare domains [66, 8, 44]. Large language models (LLMs) also support reasoning and interpretation beyond plain text, such as vision, time series, and relational databases [8, 46, 18, 45]. In jointly trained multimodal models, combining complementary signals typically improves performance over their unimodal counterparts [21]. However, this advantage may not transfer to deployment, where a modality can become unavailable due to sensor failure, incomplete data collection, privacy constraints, or system limitations [58]. Prior work on imbalanced multimodal learning shows that joint training may under-optimize modality-specific representations [37]. We study a distinct deployment-time manifestation: when one modality is completely absent at inference, a model trained with full modalities can underperform one trained only on the available modality. This setting usually arises in heterogeneous deployment environments with varying device or sensor availability [35, 40, 13].

Across diverse architectures, we observe a recurring ordering where full-modality inference performs best, unimodal training performs next, and full-modality training with a missing modality performs worst. This suggests that missing-modality degradation is not merely due to absent input information, but is also linked to internal cross-modal dependencies shaped by multimodal training. We characterize this degradation geometrically. At the parameter level, multimodal models exhibit structured principal-subspace rotations, measured by principal-angle deviations between matched multimodal and unimodal input subspaces, especially in cross-modal interaction layers. Rotation magnitude is strongly associated with the missing-modality gap. At the representation level, misclas-sified samples exhibit larger task-aware harm (negative signed task margin) than correctly classified samples at intermediate multimodal interaction stages under missing-modality inference. This gap persists toward the classifier. These localized parameter- and representation-space signatures motivate targeted parameter-level correction beyond recovery-based or adaptive methods. Motivated by these findings, we formulate missing-modality robustness as a special form of machine unlearning : attenuating harmful cross-modal dependencies under missing inputs while preserving useful multimodal functionality. We propose Geodesic Unlearning (GU) , a lightweight post-training editing method. Given a multimodal model and a matched unimodal reference, GU edits one target layer selected by an angle–harm criterion. It treats the selected principal subspace as a point on the Grassmannian , rotates its input-side basis toward the unimodal reference along a geodesic path, and reconstructs the layer while retaining the principal coefficients, output factor, and residual. Our theoretical analysis establishes the geometric optimality of this correction under a fixed subspace-distance budget and shows that the path advances all principal angles proportionally. Experiments across fusion models, CLIP-style two-tower architectures, vision-language models (VLMs), and multiple datasets further show that GU improves performance on missing-modality tasks while largely preserving full-modality accuracy. Comparisons with multimodal-specific methods, general training and adaptation baselines, and parameter-editing controls support GU’s favorable robustness–preservation trade-off, with the strongest gains when harmful dependencies are localized in editable interaction layers. In summary, our contributions are as follows: • We systematically characterize deployment-time degradation when one modality is entirely unavailable at inference across three major multimodal architectures. • We provide a geometric account of this degradation, supported by parameter- and representation-level evidence, showing that it is strongly associated with principal sub-space rotations and task-aware representational harm in cross-modal interaction layers. • We propose Geodesic Unlearning, a lightweight, theoretically grounded Grassmannian subspace editing method that applies across models to improve missing-modality robustness while preserving full-modality utility. 

## 2 Related work 

Missing Modality remains a challenge in multimodal learning [43, 68, 58]. Existing approaches address this challenge by recovering unavailable information or improving how models use available modalities. Recovery-based methods impute missing inputs at the data level [63, 22, 49, 54, 69] or representation level [35, 53, 74, 47, 12, 51], relying on cross-modal correlations and reconstruction quality. Adaptive methods accommodate missing inputs through flexible architectures [61, 50, 65], parameter adaptation [19, 73], or training–inference strategies [52, 64, 20, 60, 57, 9]. Complementary modality-balancing methods, including OGM-GE [37], AGM [30], DnR [56], and MCR [27], regulate the relative learning contributions of modalities during joint training. Our work focuses on harmful parameter dependencies retained when a modality is completely absent at deployment. We diagnose their task-relevant principal-subspace geometry and correct them in an already-trained model, providing a model-side approach that can complement training-time robustness objectives.

Machine Unlearning (MU) was originally proposed in the privacy domain to remove the influence of specific training data without full retraining [4, 3]. Existing methods span data-side interventions [67, 48], training-time updates [5, 25], decoding-time control [14, 38], and training-free parameter editing [24, 2]. Beyond privacy, MU has been explored to improve model behavior in scenarios such as continual learning [55], domain adaptation [1], and domain generalization [70]. Motivated by our finding that multimodal training induces harmful cross-modal dependencies under missing inputs, we repurpose MU to selectively attenuate these dependencies while preserving useful multimodal capability. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to formulate missing-modality robustness as an unlearning problem and address it through geometric parameter editing.

3.3 Parameter subspace geometry 

To study how multimodal training changes layer parameters, we analyze the principal singular subspaces of selected weight matrices. For a layer weight matrix W ∈ Rdo×di with SVD W =

U ΣV ⊤, we use the top-k singular vectors Uk = [ u1, . . . , u k] and Vk = [ v1, . . . , v k] to define the principal output and input subspaces, respectively. The right singular vectors Vk identify the dominant input directions the layer reads, while Uk specifies the associated output directions. Since 

Vk and VkQ span the same subspace for any orthogonal Q ∈ Rk×k, the basis-invariant object is 

S = span( Vk) ∈ Gr( k, d i); the output subspace is defined analogously on Gr( k, d o). Given a multimodal model WTI and a unimodal reference model WT, we compare their corresponding input and output subspaces to quantify how multimodal training changes parameter geometry, using standard metrics such as principal angles, subspace overlap, and Frobenius distances.

## 5 Geodesic Unlearning 

5.1 Overview 

Section 4 identifies cross-modal interaction layers as localized targets. Their principal subspace rota-tions track missing-modality degradation, and their representations exhibit task-aware harm. Given a trained multimodal model and a matched unimodal reference, we propose Geodesic Unlearning (GU) to perform a lightweight post-training correction of one selected layer. GU selects the target layer using an angle-harm criterion, rotates its principal input-side subspace toward the correspond-ing unimodal subspace along a Grassmannian geodesic, and reconstructs the layer while retaining the principal coefficients, output factor, and residual structure. This selectively attenuates harmful cross-modal dependencies under missing inputs while preserving useful multimodal functionality. The same subspace-editing procedure applies to fusion, CLIP-style two-tower, and BLIP-2 models, with only the target layer varying by architecture, enabling a unified post-training correction. 

5.2 Target-layer selection 

For each model, GU selects one target layer from the interaction and nearby projection/classifier layers analyzed in Section 4. For each candidate layer ℓ, let θℓ denote the mean principal angle between the top-k input subspaces of the TI model and T reference, and let ∆harm ,ℓ denote the final-error versus final-correct harm gap measured at layer ℓ under missing-modality evaluation. We rank layers on the development set using 

sℓ = z(θℓ) + z(∆ harm ,ℓ ), (1) 6where z(·) denotes z-score normalization across candidate layers within the same model. The layer with the largest sℓ is edited; the test set is reserved only for final evaluation.

## 6 Experiments 

6.1 Experimental setup Datasets and metrics. We evaluate GU on three multimodal benchmarks: Hateful Memes [26] for social multimodal classification, SNLI-VE [59] for visual entailment, and IU-XRay [6] for medical vision-language prediction. These three benchmarks are formulated as binary classification tasks under the deployment-time missing-modality setting. We report F1 as the primary metric to account for potential class imbalance. More dataset information is provided in Appendix E.1. We further evaluate GU on A-OKVQA [42], a multiple-choice VQA benchmark, in Appendix F.6. 

Model families. We use the model families summarized in Table 1. For each dataset and model family, we train a full multimodal model with text and image inputs, denoted as the TI model. We train a matched T reference using the same overall parameterization, where the image branch is replaced by a null input during both training and inference. This implements the T-T regime while keeping layer shapes aligned for subspace comparison and editing. 

Baselines. Our comparisons cover three groups. Multimodal-specific methods include SMIL [35], Flex-MoE [65], and DyMo [9] in a unified fusion setting. We further evaluate MoRA [73] and OGM-GE [37] on compatible architectures beyond this setting. General training and adaptation baselines include post-hoc LoRA [19] and Dual CE. Dual CE jointly supervises full-input and missing-image paths. Parameter-editing controls include layer interpolation (Weight), following parameter-space editing ideas [24], and aligned-subspace linear interpolation (Linear). These controls isolate the effects of the edited object and interpolation path. Appendix E.2 details their definitions, architecture coverage, and protocols. Additional comparisons are reported in Appendices F.4 and F.5.

6.2 Main results in the deployment-time missing-modality setting Performance. Table 2 reports mean F1 over three independent runs under the TI-T setting, where the image modality is entirely absent at inference. Corresponding sample standard deviations and supplementary ten-seed paired significance tests are provided in Appendix F.1. GU achieves the highest mean F1 among the compared methods on all three datasets, improving over the strongest baseline in each dataset by +2.38 on Hateful Memes, +1.27 on IU-XRay, and +2.21 on SNLI-VE. These consistent gains are obtained by editing only one selected layer after training. Thus, GU’s advantage comes from directly addressing the failure mode identified in Section 4: harmful cross-modal dependencies in localized parameter subspaces. The results also highlight why this deployment setting is challenging for data-side recovery and adaptive fusion methods. Many missing-modality methods are designed for settings with more available modalities and partial sample-level missingness, where the remaining modalities can still provide sufficient cross-modal information for recovery, routing, or adaptive fusion. In our setting, the system is bimodal and one of the two modalities is completely unavailable, leaving only text information. Under this stricter condition, baselines are less consistently effective: SMIL and Flex-MoE are competitive on SNLI-VE but do not consistently improve over the null-token baseline across datasets, while DyMo degrades substantially. By contrast, GU works from the model side, correcting the parameter subspace associated with harmful multimodal dependencies rather than attempting to reconstruct an unavailable modality. 6.3 Robustness–preservation trade-off across model families 

A key question is whether improving missing-modality robustness sacrifices full-modality capability. Table 3 addresses this robustness–preservation trade-off by comparing TI-T and TI-TI performance across model families. GU improves TI-T performance across model families while keeping TI-TI performance nearly unchanged, indicating that the edited models do not collapse into text-only solutions. Compared with layer interpolation, GU usually yields larger TI-T gains and smaller TI-TI degradation, suggesting that subspace-level editing better preserves useful multimodal structure than direct parameter mixing. Complete per-variant results are provided in Appendix F.3. The gain magnitude varies across architectures. GU is strongest for fusion models, where cross-modal dependencies are relatively localized in explicit fusion layers. Its gains are smaller for CLIP-style and BLIP architectures, where the relevant dependencies can be more distributed. Thus, GU is best viewed as a localized robustness correction method. It improves the robustness–preservation trade-off most when the harmful dependency is concentrated in the selected layer. 

6.4 Cost analysis 

Beyond performance, we also assess GU’s computational cost and deployment efficiency. We compare GU, post-hoc LoRA, and Dual CE on SNLI-VE using Fusion Cross-Attention, CLIP Mean Pooling, and BLIP-2 under the same hardware/software environment and evaluation protocol. LoRA starts from the same TI checkpoint as GU and adapts the same target layer with rank 8. It trains on missing-image inputs for 4 epochs at learning rate 10 −4, selecting the best checkpoint on the development set. Dual CE retrains from scratch with joint full-input and missing-image supervision. GU requires only a one-time edit before deployment, with no gradient updates to the TI checkpoint. As shown in Table 4, this stage takes 230 –858 seconds, reducing wall-clock time by 57 –88% relative to post-hoc LoRA. The resulting model introduces no additional parameters or forward-pass FLOPs relative to the original TI model. Inference uses only the edited checkpoint, without retaining the unimodal reference. For the complete offline comparison, costs start from an available TI checkpoint, excluding its original training cost. GU’s total cost includes matched reference training and editing, whereas Dual CE includes its full retraining cost. The two GU phases run sequentially, so total runtime is their sum and peak memory is the larger of their two peaks. 6.5 Ablation studies 

We ablate three key factors in GU: the geodesic coefficient η, the subspace rank k, and the target layer. As shown in Figure 4(b), GU is relatively stable across a range of η and k values, while the target layer plays a more decisive role. Varying η from 0.25 to 1.0 causes only moderate changes in missing-modality performance and full-modality preservation, while ranks from 2 to 32 yield similar TI-T performance with small TI-TI fluctuations. We therefore select hyperparameters based on the development-set trade-off between TI-T improvement and TI-TI preservation. Editing most layers yields limited gains, whereas applying GU to the selected cross-modal interaction layer attn_v_from_t_in produces the largest TI-T improvement while maintaining strong TI-TI performance. This aligns with Section 4, where subspace rotation and representational harm concentrate in cross-modal interaction stages. This result further supports the localized nature of missing-modality degradation, showing that a single well-chosen Grassmannian subspace edit can recover robustness without broadly modifying the model. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We systematically characterize a deployment-time failure mode in which full-modality multimodal models can underperform unimodal models when a modality is absent at inference. We show that this degradation is strongly associated with the principal subspace rotations of the cross-modal interaction layers and task-aware representational harm. Motivated by these findings, we propose Geodesic Unlearning, a lightweight post-training method that realigns a selected principal subspace, viewed as a point on the Grassmannian, toward a unimodal reference, with provably optimal alignment under a fixed subspace-distance budget. Experiments across model families demonstrate a favorable robustness–preservation trade-off, with further support from multi-choice VQA and missing-text evaluation. Overall, our theoretical and empirical results support a geometric view of the missing-modality problem and suggest localized subspace editing as a practical correction strategy. 10 References

Despite the importance of our work in highlighting and addressing the structural causes of missing-modality degradation in multimodal learning, there are limitations worth noting. First, our evaluation is limited to classification tasks, including multiple-choice visual question answering. Extending GU to free-form multimodal generation is an important direction for future work. Second, concerns have been raised about rendered text in Hateful Memes and hypothesis-side artifacts in SNLI-VE. We address these concerns on the affected benchmarks through targeted preprocessing and complementary controls. Further evaluations on more datasets without these specific artifacts reproduce both the degradation and GU’s improvements, confirming that neither finding requires their presence. Finally, although we cover fusion models, two-tower models, and VLMs across diverse domains, due to resource constraints, we do not conduct large-scale experiments on substantially larger models. 

## B Broader impacts 

This work improves the robustness of multimodal models to missing modality inputs, which is impor-tant in real-world settings where inputs may be incomplete due to sensor failure, privacy constraints, or deployment limitations. Such improvements may benefit applications like medical decision support and multimodal content understanding. However, increased robustness may encourage deployment in settings where incorrect predictions can still occur. In addition, robustness does not eliminate biases present in training data.

E.2 Baselines 

For experimental organization, we group the compared methods into multimodal-specific methods, general training and adaptation baselines, and parameter-editing controls. This grouping reflects each method’s role and applicability in the evaluation, rather than a mutually exclusive taxonomy of prior work. Coverage follows each method’s architectural requirements: SMIL, Flex-MoE, and DyMo are compared under a matched fusion backbone, while additional architecture-compatible methods extend the evaluation to other model families. 22 E.2.1 Multimodal-specific methods SMIL, Flex-MoE, and DyMo. We evaluate SMIL [35], Flex-MoE [65], and DyMo [9] on all three main datasets using the unified RoBERTa–ResNet50 cross-attention fusion backbone. This choice follows the structural assumptions of their released implementations. SMIL couples Bayesian meta-learning with missing-modality feature reconstruction, requiring choices of reconstruction targets and auxiliary-network insertion points. Flex-MoE uses a missing-modality bank, experts, and specialized routers; DyMo uses recovered-modality candidates and inference-time modality selection. Extending these methods to CLIP-style or BLIP-2 models is possible, but would require method-specific changes to reconstruction targets, native fusion or Q-Former components, or routing and recovery modules. Such redesigns would alter the architecture, parameter budget, and optimization pipeline, adding design choices to matched-backbone comparisons. This setting provides a common backbone and training protocol while retaining each method’s auxiliary components. For SMIL, Flex-MoE, and DyMo, we use the official codebases released by the original papers and keep unspecified settings consistent with their default configurations. To ensure a fair comparison, we use the same number of training epochs across our models: 10 for Hateful Memes, 10 for IU-XRay, and 4 for SNLI-VE. We also match the main learning rates of these baselines to our unified cross-attention fusion setting. The only exception is the SMIL inner-loop learning rate, which we set to 1.0 × 10 −3 to follow the official implementation. 

MoRA and OGM-GE. MoRA [73] provides a missing-modality adaptation baseline on CLIP Late Concat and Mean Pooling across the three main datasets. Its modality-specific and shared low-rank updates couple paired text and vision encoder weights; applying this formulation to Fusion Concat would require new insertion points and a fusion-specific sharing rule. OGM-GE [37] provides a training-time modality-balancing baseline on Fusion Concat and both CLIP variants across the same datasets. CLIP Mean Pooling permits an exact decomposition of the full logit into text and image contributions. For nonlinear Late Concat, modality confidence is estimated through native unimodal forward paths while retaining the published discrepancy, gradient-modulation, and generalization-enhancement rules. Results are reported in Appendix F.4.1. SiMO [57] uses a specialized collaborative-perception architecture and staged training. Porting it to our setting would replace the evaluated backbone and training pipeline, rather than provide a matched-backbone comparison. 

E.2.2 General training and adaptation baselines Dual CE. On SNLI-VE, we retrain Fusion Cross-Attention, CLIP Mean Pooling, and BLIP-2 from scratch with a shared classifier jointly supervising full-input and missing-image paths: 

LDual CE = LTI + 0 .5 LT.

Both terms use binary cross-entropy with logits, evaluated with full text–image inputs and with the image replaced by a null input, respectively. Dual CE+GU applies GU to the resulting checkpoint to test whether the localized edit complements joint supervision. The comparison is reported in Appendix F.4.2. 

LoRA. For the SNLI-VE comparison on the same three architectures, post-hoc LoRA [19] starts from the same TI checkpoint as GU and adapts the same target layer on missing-image inputs. We use rank 8, train for 4 epochs at learning rate 10 −4, and select the best checkpoint on the development set. Performance and computational costs are reported in Section 6.4.

W ′ = (1 − α)WTI + αW T.

We fix α = 0 .5 for all datasets and models. All experiments can be run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80GB of memory. F.5 Controlled interventions for geometric correction 

We examine two design choices underlying GU: where to edit and how to edit . These controlled comparisons connect the diagnostics in Section 4 with the geometric rationale in Appendix D.2. 

F.5.1 Target-layer selection 

Using the IU-XRay Fusion Cross-Attention layer sweep in Figure 4(b), we compare angle-only, harm-only, and joint angle–harm selection while keeping checkpoints and the editing protocol fixed. Angle-only and harm-only rank candidate layers by principal-subspace deviation and task-aware harm, respectively, while the joint rule uses the development-set score in Eq. (1). Table 22 reports mean ± sample standard deviation over three independent runs. ∆T and ∆T I denote changes in TI-T and TI-TI relative to the original TI model. The joint rule produces the largest TI-T gain ( +1 .41 F1) and smallest TI-TI loss ( −0.13 F1). Angle-only and harm-only selection yield smaller gains of +0 .34 and +0 .72 , with TI-TI changes of −0.61 

and −0.36 , respectively. Combining geometric deviation with task-aware harm therefore identifies a more effective correction target than either signal alone. 

F.5.2 Editing strategy comparison 

We next compare three editing strategies on Fusion Cross-Attention across the three datasets. All methods use matched TI/T checkpoints, the same target layer and reference, and the same evaluation protocol. Weight interpolates the full selected weight matrix with its reference using fixed α = 0 .5,thereby changing input orientation together with output factors, scales, and residual. Linear and GU 

instead edit the aligned principal input subspace while retaining the original TI output factor, principal coefficients, and residual. Linear applies polar-retracted interpolation as in Eq. (13), whereas GU follows the Grassmannian geodesic. Both use k = 8 and η = 0 .75 . Table 23 reports TI-T / TI-TI F1 as mean ± sample standard deviation over three independent runs. GU achieves the highest mean TI-T and TI-TI in all three settings. On HM and IU-XRay, it improves both missing-image recovery and full-modality preservation over Weight and Linear.
