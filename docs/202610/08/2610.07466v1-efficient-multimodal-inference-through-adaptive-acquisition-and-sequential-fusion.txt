ABSTRACT 

Multimodal systems often encode every available input, even when a subset suffices for prediction. Adaptive acquisition can reduce this cost by using predictions from incrementally fused evidence to decide which modality to encode next and when to stop . However, sequential fusion makes these predictions order-dependent, so decisions based on them may need to distinguish factorially many histories of the same acquired set. We introduce S EMARC, which couples a Sequential Modal-ity Aggregator (S EMA) with an Adaptive Runtime Controller (ARC) and uses acquired evidence to select each modality before its encoder runs. S EMA executes only selected encoder and fusion branches, updates a fixed-size state, and predicts after each acquisition without recomputing earlier branches. We supervise every acquisition prefix under randomized modality subsets and orders to encourage consistent predictions across acquisition orders. ARC combines a set-dependent marginal-utility prior with residual fitted-Q learning to select the next available modality or stop, without inspecting unacquired inputs or retaining acquisition order. Across six multimodal classification datasets and eleven baselines, S E-MARC achieves 3.2% higher macro-F1 and 61.4% lower total inference GFLOPs on average relative to each dataset’s most accurate baseline. End-to-end latency falls by 44.0% across GPU and CPU and by 47.2% on Android INT8 relative to the fastest measured baseline, on average. Under varying runtime modality missingness, S EMARC still skips available modalities, matching or exceeding the best baseline macro-F1 in 21 of 24 conditions with 14.8% lower total GFLOPs on average. S EMARC thus offers a practical path toward efficient multimodal inference across heterogeneous devices.

This motivates adaptive runtime estimation of modality value from acquired evidence, enabling the system to select the next modality or stop before incurring unnecessary computation. But translating selective use into computational savings requires attention to when selection occurs (Figure 1a–c) and how the fusion is carried out subsequently. Selective fusion based on encoded features or lightweight modality previews can reduce downstream computation (Xue & Marculescu, 2023; Panda et al., 2021). However, it still incurs the cost of obtaining those modality features, including from modalities ultimately discarded. Active feature acquisition works address which inputs to query and when to stop (Shim et al., 2018), but reducing acquisition cost alone does not ensure reuse of multimodal computation across successive modalities. Motivated by these complementary directions, we propose to couple modality acquisition decisions with incremental multimodal fusion: selecting each modality before executing its encoder and fusion branch for every sample, reusing accumulated computation as evidence arrives, and stopping when further processing is no longer worthwhile. We propose SEMARC , a framework for sample-adaptive modality acquisition and incremental multimodal fusion (Figures 1c and 3). The system must choose the next modality or stop while reusing earlier computation. Yet incremental fusion can yield different predictions across orders of the same acquired evidence, and explicitly accounting for this dependence can require distinguishing factorially many acquisition histories. Our Sequential Modality Aggregator (S EMA) reuses a compact bottleneck state and predicts after each acquisition. Training combines a modality-dropout curriculum, randomized orders, and prediction supervision with distillation at each step, encouraging consistent predictions across orders of the same acquired evidence. We relate prediction disagreement to prefix risk and use the approximate information state framework to characterize when a compact summary can serve as an approximately Markovian state. Our Adaptive Runtime Controller (ARC) 

uses current predictions, acquired-set identity, and modality availability without explicitly retaining acquisition order. Trained on the frozen S EMA, it combines an offline, set-dependent marginal-utility prior with standard residual fitted-Q learning to select the next modality or stop. At runtime, each acquisition executes only the selected encoder and fusion branch, updating the prediction for the next decision without rerunning earlier branches or processing unselected modalities. Our contributions are threefold:  

> ▶

Coupled incremental fusion and acquisition. We introduce S EMARC, which couples incremen-tal bottleneck fusion (S EMA) with an acquisition controller (ARC) through prefix predictions trained to be consistent across acquisition orders. Acquisition and stopping decisions govern incremental encoder and fusion execution, reusing earlier computation and leaving unselected branches unexecuted.  

> ▶

Design rationale and supporting analysis. We examine order sensitivity in sequential fusion and motivate randomized-order prefix supervision. Supporting analysis relates population prediction risk to order disagreement and separately identifies the additional assumptions required for compact-state control.  

> ▶

Under 10 to 40% test-time missingness, it retains the best macro-F1 in 21 of 24 conditions while skipping modalities that are present, at 14.8% lower total GFLOPs than the best baseline, so the savings come from selection rather than 2Preprint from missing inputs alone. Further component replacements, ablations, and diagnostics examine the proposed coupled design and acquisition behavior. 

2 RELATED WORKS 

Efficient and incomplete multimodal inference. Multimodal Bottleneck Transformer (MBT) (Na-grani et al., 2021) limits cross-modal communication through bottleneck tokens; ShaSpec (Wang et al., 2023) and MAESTRO (Mohapatra et al., 2025) handle incomplete inputs; MMEE (Hamed et al., 2024) exits at early layer; DyMo (Du et al., 2026) selects reliable recovered modalities. AdaMML (Panda et al., 2021) selects modalities from inexpensive previews; DynMM (Xue & Marculescu, 2023) gates modality/fusion paths; HCMS (Weng et al., 2023) gates costly modalities in an audio-first hierarchy; multimodal early exiting (Hamed et al., 2024) adapts depth. Our S EMARC selects each subsequent available modality from acquired evidence before encoding and fusion, reusing accumulated state. 

Static and adaptive modality acquisition. Greedy selection (Cheng et al., 2022; He et al., 2024) uses approximate submodularity for cardinality-constrained, dataset-level modality subsets. Joint-AFA (Shim et al., 2018) jointly learns cost-sensitive acquisition, stopping, and classification with order-invariant set encoding. A2MT (Kossen et al., 2022) schedules observations per timestep using predictive feedback over a fixed horizon, balancing prediction quality and acquisition costs while excluding computation costs. Concurrent, independently developed EAMA (Peroni et al., 2026) iteratively acquires unobserved modality subsets by estimated utility, stopping when none offers positive net utility. Complementing these methods, S EMARC couples acquisition and incremental fusion per sample, using each updated prefix prediction to choose one whole modality branch or STOP without rerunning earlier branches. 

Sequential fusion and order robustness. MoDN (Trottet et al., 2023) and MultiModN (Swamy et al., 2023) reuse state for intermediate predictions; MultiModN skips absent modules, evaluates a fixed order, and discusses permutation training. Deep Sets (Zaheer et al., 2017) constructs invariant set functions; Janossy pooling (Murphy et al., 2018) studies symmetrization of order-sensitive functions, including random permutation training. S EMARC trains sequential fusion over randomized modality subsets and orders with prefix supervision, relates expected prediction disagreement to excess prefix cross-entropy risk, and evaluates only the realized acquisition path at inference. Separately, we specialize the approximate information state framework (Subramanian et al., 2022) to characterize the additional reward and action-conditioned transition assumptions needed for control from compact predictive summaries. 

3 METHODOLOGY 

We aim to improve inference efficiency by selectively processing available modalities while preserving predictive performance. For each sample x, let A ⊆ { 1, . . . , M } denote the available modalities. A controller decides which available modality to process next and when to stop, using only acquired evidence and modality availability. These decisions precede the execution of each selected branch, avoiding the cost of encoding and fusing unselected modalities. Incremental fusion enables these sequential decisions to reuse previous computation, but can make predictions depend on acquisition order. Ideally, different processing orders of the same sample and acquired modality set should yield consistent predictions. This motivates co-designing the fusion network and controller around an order-robust summary of the acquired evidence. SEMARC addresses these requirements by coupling S EMA, which incrementally fuses selected modalities and is trained for order-robust prediction after each acquisition, with ARC, which uses the resulting summary and modality availability to select the next modality or stop. 4 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

We evaluate S EMARC against eleven multimodal baselines on six datasets, covering full and simulated partial modality availability and heterogeneous compute platforms. 

Datasets. We evaluate S EMARC on six multimodal time-series classification benchmarks: IEMO-CAP (Busso et al., 2008), MM-Fi (Yang et al., 2023), CMI (Child Mind Institute, 2025), CZU-MHAD (Chao et al., 2022), EAV (Lee et al., 2024), and UTD-MHAD (Chen et al., 2015). Training, validation, and test subjects are disjoint within each evaluation split. EAV uses three independently randomized cross-subject splits; test subjects may overlap across runs. Appendix B provides full preparation details and the other dataset protocols, including rebalanced splits. 

Baselines. We compare S EMARC with eleven baselines grouped by selection timing. Monolithic fusion : MAESTRO , ShaSpec , MBT and DecAlign (Qian et al., 2026) all fuse every available input. 

Fixed-sequential fusion : MultiModN processes externally available modalities sequentially without learning their order or stopping. Selective fusion : Greedy Modality Selection (GMS) chooses a dataset-level subset under a fixed budget; AdaMML selects per segment using multimodal previews; 

DynMM routes through data-dependent fusion paths; DyMo selects among recovered modalities; and an early exit baseline MMEE which exits along a predetermined computation graph. None selects the next unseen modality from accumulated prefix evidence with learned stopping. Proactive sequential acquisition : Joint-AFA learns feature acquisition and stopping; we treat each modality as a feature group. Baselines use published hyperparameters, with search for unspecified settings. We report mean macro-F1, GFLOPs, latency, and energy in the main text; full statistics and implementation details are given in Appendices E and C, respectively. Per-sample compute covers the full path from raw modality streams to logits or, when explicitly labeled GF f , only the post modality-encoding to logits stage; Appendix D specifies selector accounting. 4.1 PREDICTIVE PERFORMANCE AND INFERENCE EFFICIENCY 

Higher F1 at lower average compute. SEMARC achieves the highest mean macro-F1 on four of six datasets (Table 1). On CMI, the F1 is 0.556 versus 0.561 for DecAlign, while total compute falls from 23.33 to 0.43 GFLOPs. On MM-Fi, total computation falls from 18,098 GFLOPs for AdaMML to 738 GFLOPs for S EMARC, a reduction of approximately 95.9%; their post-extraction costs are 0.17 and 0.07 GFLOPs, respectively. By selecting each modality before executing its branch, S EMARC avoids extraction for unselected inputs and uses approximately 47.9% of the available modalities on average. 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced S EMARC, coupling incremental fusion with adaptive acquisition and stopping so that acquired evidence determines subsequent computation. Across six datasets, S EMARC achieves 3.2% higher macro-F 1 and 61.4% lower total GFLOPs on average than each dataset’s most accurate baseline under full availability, with practical latency gains across devices and continued computational savings under modality missingness. 

Limitations and future work. Our evaluation focuses on per-sample classification with fixed modality costs and batch-one inference. Our compact-state analysis provides conditional guarantees under reward and transition approximation assumptions; empirically assessing these assumptions and extending the analysis to the fitted neural controller remain directions for future work. Future work includes adapting acquisition to externally supplied, time-varying deployment costs, designing batching and scheduling for higher throughput, and incorporating memory across samples to support long-horizon tasks. 9Preprint

A IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS OF THE SEMARC POLICY 

The backbone is frozen before policy fitting. Cached ordered-prefix predictions define the transition samples, the class-conditional gain tables define the prior Q0, and a residual network is fitted with Double-DQN targets (Section 3.2). All of the following is per dataset and per fold. 

Backbone configuration. Table 3 lists the dataset-specific S EMA configurations. All use d = 128 ,8 heads, K = 16 bottleneck tokens, dense attention, running-mean aggregation (Section 3.1), and randomized modality order. We train with AdamW (weight decay 0), a cosine learning-rate schedule from the listed initial rate to 10 −6, and gradient clipping at norm 1. Batch-level modality dropout retains at least one modality; its probability is 0 for the first Ew epochs, increases linearly over the next Er epochs, and remains at pmax thereafter. Each forward pass uniformly permutes the retained modalities. We optimize Equation 1 with λds = 1 .0, λkd = 0 .5, and uniform prefix weights. For each forward pass, let n be the number of retained modalities. The distillation target is ¯p = sg( pn),the detached prediction after processing all n retained modalities in the same sampled order and forward pass. Each prefix prediction is distilled toward this target, with gradients stopped through 

¯p. The loss ℓ is cross-entropy; on IEMOCAP, it uses class weights N/ (CN c) computed from the training labels. We train one checkpoint per fold with the listed seed and retain the epoch with the best validation macro-F1. Prefix cache. With M modalities, we run the frozen backbone once over every ordered modality prefix of every sample in each split (train, validation, test) and store the class posterior after each prefix. The cache is a complete depth-M prefix tree with PMk=0 M !/(M − k)! nodes: 1957 for IEMOCAP ( M =6 ), 13 700 for CMI and CZU-MHAD ( M =7 ), 326 for MM-Fi ( M =5 ), 65 for UTD-MHAD ( M =4 ) and 16 for EAV ( M =3 ). The residual MLP is still evaluated, but the backbone is not rerun during policy fitting or selection. Test-cache entries are reserved for final evaluation. 

Data used at each stage. The policy is fitted entirely on the validation fold, because the backbone’s training-split predictions are overfit (correct at almost every prefix) and carry no acquisition signal. The validation samples are split once into a fit part and a holdout part: the N validation indices are permuted with NumPy’s default_rng(1000) , the first max(5 , ⌊0.2N ⌋) form the holdout and the rest the fit part, the same split for every seed, price and variant. The gain tables TS and the residual network are fitted on the fit part; the holdout part selects the FQI iteration to keep; the seed is selected on the validation fold; the test fold is touched once, for the reported numbers. The train split is not used by the policy at all.

B COMPLETE DATASET PREPARATION 

B.1 DATASET PREPARATION 

This section details the six datasets used for evaluation: modalities and their native sampling rates, the feature each modality is mapped to before fusion, class distributions, and the cross-subject splits. The train, validation and test partitions are subject-disjoint. Backbone and policy fitting and model selection use the designated training and validation data. Quantization calibration uses val-split examples, as specified in Appendix F.1. 

Common preprocessing. Each modality is converted to a sequence (Tm, d m), either directly from the raw stream (sensor and motion-capture channels) or through a frozen extractor (DINOv3 ViT-L/16 (Siméoni et al., 2025) for image and depth frames, XLS-R (Babu et al., 2021) or wav2vec 2.0 (Baevski et al., 2020) for audio, BERT-base (Devlin et al., 2019) for text, and a frozen, randomly initialized PointNet (Qi et al., 2017) descriptor for point clouds). Preprocessing includes setting non-finite entries to zero, normalization by the largest per-timestep ℓ2 norm within each sequence, and temporal alignment to a common length T . The default temporal alignment uses zero-padding when shorter and linear interpolation when longer; the CZU-MHAD resampling and normalization order is specified below. T = 100 on IEMOCAP and T = 128 elsewhere. Per-modality extraction times for the explicitly labelled cost-weighted variants are measured on the same hardware for every dataset; the default policy uses uniform acquisition costs. 

Extractor checkpoints and feature layers. All extractors are frozen. Image, audio, and text extractors use publicly released pretrained checkpoints without fine-tuning; the PointNet descriptors use fixed random weights, as specified below. The same frozen extractor weights produce the training features and server-side measurements and serve as the source for the ONNX graphs deployed on the phone. The extraction environment uses PyTorch 2.5.1, torchaudio 2.5.1, and Transformers 5.3.0. 

Images and depth. DINOv3 ViT-L/16 uses the official repository’s 

dinov3_vitl16_pretrain_lvd1689m weights, pretrained on LVD-1689M. Each frame is represented by the 1024-dimensional CLS token after the final LayerNorm ( x_norm_clstoken ). Frames are resized directly to 224 × 224 with bilinear interpolation, without center cropping, and ImageNet-normalized. RGB video frames are used as decoded. The 16-bit depth maps from MM-Fi and UTD-MHAD are clipped to 0.1–6 m, min–max scaled, and replicated to three channels; CZU-MHAD’s 8-bit depth frames are replicated to three channels.

Among AdaMML, DynMM and DyMo, AdaMML and DyMo can skip encoders, whereas DynMM cannot. MultiModN skips a missing modality’s state update but still executes its encoder. Vanilla MBT is a plain Multimodal Bottleneck Transformer: the same shared-bottleneck-token fusion our backbone uses, with four bottleneck tokens introduced at layer three, but with no sequential ordering, no early exit and no policy. It is the non-adaptive counterpart of our fusion at identical width and depth. 19 Preprint 

MAESTRO is run from the authors’ released model with every layer, buffer and parameter name unchanged, so the published checkpoints load strictly. It keeps its own ProbSparse attention, mixture-of-experts feed-forward and modality-dropout schedule with a learned dynamic factor; the only change is the input adapter that splits our concatenated per-modality tensor. 

ShaSpec uses the original implementation, including its shared and modality-specific representations and missing-modality mechanism. The surrogate shared representation is the mean of the available shared features. 

DecAlign is generalised from its original three modalities (text, audio, video) to M modalities. The heterogeneity alignment scales accordingly: the multi-marginal Sinkhorn transport runs over an 

M -dimensional cost tensor, and the directed cross-attention branch has M (M − 1) pairs. Everything else, including the three-path fusion and the decoupling loss, is as published. 

MultiModN is a faithful port of the sequential modular network: a shared state vector updated one modality at a time by an un-gated MLP that concatenates the modality feature with the running state, and a shared decoder readable after every prefix. Its per-modality encoder is the same six-layer Transformer as the other baselines, and all prefixes are supervised during training. A missing modality skips its state update, as in the original mechanism; raw-data extraction is skipped, while its downstream encoder still processes the zero-filled input. Because the model is order-sensitive, we report it in the order chosen on validation by exhaustive search over all M ! orders. 

Modality-selective baselines. These make a per-sample decision about which modalities to process, and are measured live: the same forward pass that produces the prediction is what is timed and whose executed-path compute is reported. Under missingness, raw-data extraction is skipped for absent modalities, and unavailable actions are masked. Downstream encoder skipping is method-specific: AdaMML and DyMo can skip encoders, whereas DynMM retains encoder execution. 

AdaMML keeps the official policy network: a lightweight network previews a cheap summary of the input and emits a per-modality keep/drop decision by hard, straight-through Gumbel-softmax with an annealed temperature. Two changes, both forced by the setting. The decision is made once per sample rather than per segment through an LSTM, since our inputs are single windows rather than long videos. The main network is a set of per-modality encoders with logit-level late fusion under learned weights, so that a dropped modality’s encoder is genuinely skipped at inference and the saving is real. Training follows the original three phases: encoder warm-up, joint policy and encoder training, and fine-tuning. 

DynMM mirrors the official affect-recognition implementation: nested modality-subset experts, a per-sample gate trained with the FLOP-resource regulariser as its only auxiliary loss, and no distillation. Each expert is a set of MultiBench-style Transformer encoders with concatenation fusion. We use the two-branch configuration, a single-modality expert and a full expert, with the resource loss weight at 0.1 and a soft gate during training that is made hard at inference. 

DyMo uses the original implementation, retaining its missing-modality recovery and per-sample selection mechanisms on the repository’s cross-modal Transformer backbone. The configuration uses prototype-distance quality with cosine temperature 0.1, per-subset Gaussian probabilities, the prototype training loss, per-epoch prototype recomputation as class means of projected class-token features, and greedy per-sample selection with reward R(u) = quality (S ∪ { u}) G − quality (S),with K = 4 and a 16-dimensional projection. Missing raw streams are not extracted, and the implementation can skip downstream encoders. 

Early-exit baseline. MMEE ports the anytime early-exit mechanism, off-ramp heads on intermediate fusion layers with weighted multi-exit training, onto a generic concatenate-then-fuse Transformer. It exits in the depth axis, complementary to our exit in the modality axis, at the first head whose maximum softmax reaches 0.9. All available modalities are extracted, while missing raw streams are skipped. Downstream modality encoders still execute on zero-filled inputs for absent modalities. Its usage is 1.0 relative to the available modalities; early exit reduces fusion depth rather than selecting modalities.

MultiModN+ARC attaches our acquisition policy to the MultiModN backbone instead of ours, with the prefix cache rebuilt from MultiModN’s own per-prefix decoder. This tests whether the policy’s gains transfer to a backbone that was not trained to be order-robust. 

Own-backbone variants. For the main table, greedy submodular selection and JAFA are also run end to end with their own classifiers trained from scratch, so that each method owns its full pipeline as its paper assumes. Both use the same neutral per-modality encoder, two 1D convolutions with global average pooling, which is EAMA’s stated time-series encoder, the natural sequence analogue of JAFA’s per-element embedder, and a mild strengthening of the MLP Cheng et al. use. For greedy selection, H(Y | S) is estimated by the converged training loss of a model trained on S, as in the paper’s Section 4.2, with one model per budget. JAFA is trained as the released code trains it: classifier pre-training on Bernoulli(0.5) subsets, then joint training with ϵ-greedy episodes, terminal reward log p(y | acquired ), cost 0.05 per acquisition, smooth-L1 loss, periodic target copies and double DQN. Under missingness, a selected modality that is absent is not acquired and its encoder is skipped; JAFA’s episodes mask unavailable actions. 21 Preprint 

Ours without ARC. The backbone alone, consuming every available modality in the fold’s validation leave-one-out order and predicting once. This provides a full-modality reference for evaluating the policy’s accuracy–cost trade-off. 

Missingness protocol. Test-time missingness is simulated by dropping each modality of each sample independently with probability ρ ∈ { 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4}, re-instating one uniformly chosen modality for any sample that would lose all of them, with a fixed seed per fold. Every method sees the same masks. All methods skip raw-data extraction for absent modalities. Fixed-width baselines process zero-filled features in their downstream encoders; AdaMML and DyMo can skip encoders, whereas DynMM cannot. Selective baselines and our policy are forbidden from choosing an unavailable modality. MU uses the number of available modalities for each sample as its denominator.

Hardware and runtime. On-device measurements use a OnePlus phone with a Qualcomm Snap-dragon 8 Gen 3 (SM8650: one Cortex-X4, five Cortex-A720 and two Cortex-A520 cores, Adreno 750 GPU, and Hexagon NPU), 16 GB RAM, and Android 16. One Kotlin application performs the complete pipeline at batch size 1: raw-file decoding, feature extraction, modality acquisition, fusion, and prediction. Inference uses ONNX Runtime for Android 1.19 ( onnxruntime-android from Maven), with the CPU execution provider and four intra-op threads. NNAPI, GPU and NPU execution providers are disabled. Encoder sessions are created once and remain resident, excluding session creation from timed samples. Latency is measured with System.nanoTime . Phone energy is calculated from the battery fuel gauge by integrating instantaneous current times voltage at 10 Hz and subtracting an idle baseline calibrated for 3 s before each run, with the phone unplugged and the screen on. For datasets whose samples are shorter than the gauge update interval, all measured samples are timed as one block and total latency and energy are divided by the sample count. To keep measurement time manageable, we perform measurements on a subset of the test samples. Every method uses the same fixed first 50 test samples of each fold, with per-sample records retained in the app’s result files. On-device macro-F1 is computed on these samples; server GPU/CPU macro-F1 uses the complete test folds. The quantization pilot below reports accuracy and is distinct from macro-F1. Thus, server and phone prediction metrics describe different evaluation populations. 

Quantization. All deployed methods use post-training quantization without fine-tuning. Deploy-ment uses ONNX Runtime dynamic W8A8 quantization, with per-channel INT8 weights and runtime per-tensor INT8 activations for compute-dominant matrix multiplications, including linear and at-tention projections. Normalization, softmax, activation functions, and small Gemm /Conv layers remain fp32. SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023) redistributes activation outliers into weights through an equivalent fp32 transformation. 

Calibration data and label use. Calibration inputs come from the validation partitions. The initial IEMOCAP calibration for XLS-R, BERT, DINOv3, and the fusion graph uses eight samples from the val manifest, sampled with seed 0 and stratified by label (two samples per emotion). Fusion graphs for the other five datasets use the last eight samples in each val-fold manifest. The shared DINOv3 image encoder is subsequently recalibrated on a mixture of IEMOCAP video frames and frames from the first three staged val samples per source on UTD-MHAD (depth and RGB), CZU-MHAD (depth), and MM-Fi (depth), using 12 frames per source. SmoothQuant calibration itself uses channel-wise maxima of absolute input activations for each matrix multiplication and weight ranges, without a supervised loss or parameter fine-tuning. Dynamic W8A8 computes activation scales per tensor at runtime. Table 19 motivates this choice. Whole-stack accuracy on IEMOCAP varies by less than 0.5 percentage points across precisions, but deployment efficiency and feature fidelity differ substantially. fp16 causes ViT-L attention overflow and largely falls back to fp32 on the CPU; weight-only INT8 preserves fidelity but provides little speedup. W8A8 enables fast INT8 kernels, yet severely distorts DINOv3 and BERT features. SmoothQuant improves their cosine similarity to fp32 from 0.618 to 0.990 and from 0.938 to 0.979, respectively, while retaining accelerated inference. Fusion graphs operating on ℓ2-normalized features already preserve outputs under either variant (mean cosine 0.9999; argmax agreement 1.000). Across the adaptive methods, SmoothQuant increases end-to-end prediction agreement with fp32 from 81.2% to 93.8%, with exact agreement for S EMARC and DyMo (24 fold-0 IEMOCAP samples run end to end through the ONNX graphs, S EMARC under the deployed rule). We therefore deploy SmoothQuant W8A8 without fine-tuning for every method.

Experimental Setup for Missingness Study. We evaluate every method on the same six datasets and the same cross-subject test folds as Table 1, under test-time modality missingness at rates 

ρ ∈ { 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4}; ρ = 0 is the full-availability setting of the main table. Missingness is simulated per test sample: each of the M modalities is dropped independently with probability ρ,and if every modality of a sample is dropped one is reinstated uniformly at random. The masks are drawn once per fold with a fixed seed and shared by all methods, so every method sees exactly the same set of absent modalities on every sample. All models use the checkpoints of the full-availability experiment, and S EMARC keeps its deployed acquisition policy unchanged across all levels. The policy is trained with modality-availability augmentation and absent modalities are in-distribution for it at test time. Every method skips raw-data extraction for absent modalities. MBT, MAESTRO, ShaSpec, DecAlign, MultiModN and MMEE still execute their downstream modality encoders with zero-filled inputs for absent positions; MultiModN separately skips the corresponding state updates. AdaMML and DyMo can skip encoders, whereas DynMM cannot. GMS, Joint-AFA and S EMARC request only available modalities through their selection or acquisition masks. MU is normalized per sample by the number of available modalities, and is therefore 1 for methods that retain all available inputs. End-to-end GFLOPs include the raw extraction actually performed and the downstream encoders, fusion, and controller computation executed at batch size 1. Macro-F1 is computed on the full test set of each fold and averaged over folds; standard deviations are given where all folds were measured under the three-trial protocol.
