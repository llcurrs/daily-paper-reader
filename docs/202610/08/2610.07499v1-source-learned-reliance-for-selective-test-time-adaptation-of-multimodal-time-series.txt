## ABSTRACT 

Multimodal wearable systems must remain reliable when sensor streams become noisy or unavailable. Existing multimodal test-time adaptation (TTA) methods often assess reliability online, but cross-modal agreement can be misleading when sensors measure different physical processes, and evaluating alternative modal-ity configurations adds inference cost. We propose CARAT , which decouples model reliance from runtime corruption detection to guide omission or attenuation, amortizing reliance estimation through source training. An asymmetric modality-dropout curriculum prepares a missingness-resilient backbone for omission and derives a frozen, backbone-specific reliance proxy from windowed input-projection gradient norms. At deployment, a lightweight one-class detector flags suspect streams, and the proxy guides a joint choice between replacing the suspect set with the backbone’s trained missingness symbol and attenuating its representations before fusion, without candidate-subset evaluation. Across four wearable datasets, five corruption types, three backbones, and eight TTA baselines, CARAT achieves the highest overall macro-F1 and best mean rank (2.42), exceeding EATA, the strongest baseline, by 1.58 F1 points across 12 equally weighted dataset–backbone settings. Across five profiled configurations, CARAT uses 9.49% fewer GFLOPs and updates 47.82% fewer parameters than EATA. A pattern also emerges across sensing regimes: multimodal TTA methods such as PTA are competitive on IMU-dominated homogeneous datasets, whereas unimodal TTA methods like TENT and EATA match or exceed it on heterogeneous datasets. These results position 

CARAT as a practical default to wearable TTA, offering competitive robustness with modest computational requirements and benefits that vary across backbones and dataset regimes. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal wearable systems combine several sensor streams to support applications such as human activity recognition and stress monitoring (Altun et al., 2010; Reiss et al., 2019; Schmidt et al., 2018; Mohapatra et al., 2024a). These applications can involve numerous motion and physiological sensing streams, each with distinct physical characteristics and sources of noise. At deployment, missingness, sensor failure, motion artifacts, drift, and nonstationary noise can affect individual streams or several streams simultaneously (Zhang et al., 2014; Sharma et al., 2007). Maintaining reliable predictions therefore requires handling changes in both the availability and quality of these inputs. Test-time adaptation (TTA) addresses deployment shifts by updating pretrained models using unla-beled test data (Wang et al., 2020a; Niu et al., 2022), with multimodal extensions targeting reliability imbalance and selective adaptation (Yang et al., 2024; Guo & Jin, 2025; Chen et al., 2025). These methods are evaluated on two or three modalities of one kind, such as audio, video, and optical flow (Yang et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2025), and decide which stream is unreliable either by compar-ing streams against each other, which fails when sensors measure different physical processes (an accelerometer and a skin-temperature sensor disagree by design), or by trying alternative modality configurations at inference, which costs extra forward passes. Wearable systems carry many streams of different kinds (ten in WESAD (Schmidt et al., 2018)), which makes both problems worse.

Because they are trained to predict from incomplete inputs, their missing-input operation can be reused to deliberately omit suspect streams during adaptation. However, just supporting missing inputs does not establish when omission is beneficial. Removing a corrupted stream may suppress harmful inputs, but it can also discard useful information that the remaining modalities cannot replace. Choosing between omission and attenuation (keeping a stream but downweighting it before fusion) therefore requires considering the backbone’s reliance on both the suspect and remaining streams, that is, how much its predictions depend on each. The next challenge is to make this choice without the costly deployment-time probing. With K

suspect streams, unrestricted retain/remove choices admit 2K subsets; assessing these alternatives requires repeated predictions, and even leave-one-modality-out tests add inference work. Source training can provide an opportunity to estimate modality reliance before deployment. A source-derived reliance proxy can summarize how the backbone learned to use its modalities, while a detector assesses current input abnormality. Combining these signals offers a way to guide omission and attenuation without evaluating candidate subsets online. 

Our Approach. We propose CARAT (Conditional modality composition and reliance-guided 

at tenuation for efficient test-time adaptation). The main idea is: during source training, the backbone learns to predict with missing streams and, as a by-product, how much it relies on each one. At test time, a detector flags suspect streams; if the backbone relies mostly on the healthy streams, the suspect ones are dropped, otherwise they are kept but turned down (Figure 1). An asymmetric modality-dropout curriculum uses windowed gradient norms at modality-specific input projections to prepare the backbone for missing inputs and derive a frozen, backbone-specific reliance proxy. For each sample, the share of the reliance profile assigned to unflagged modalities determines whether to replace the entire suspect set with the trained missingness symbol or attenuate its encoder outputs through learnable scalars before fusion. Only normalization and attenuation parameters are updated using a sample-weighted entropy objective. The key contributions are:

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Multimodal test-time adaptation. TTA updates pretrained models using unlabeled deployment data (Wang et al., 2020a; Niu et al., 2022; Gong et al., 2023). READ (Yang et al., 2024) combines self-adaptive attention with a confidence-aware objective; SuMi (Guo & Jin, 2025) uses sample selection, unimodal assistance, and information sharing; PTA (Wang et al.) combines prediction-bias reweighting with attention alignment; and AEO (Dong et al., 2025) optimizes entropy and modality-prediction discrepancies for open-set adaptation. Closest to our intervention design, TSA (Chen et al., 2025) learns modality-specific adapters and a router at test time for single-modality shifts, evaluating alternative adapter configurations at inference. Source training for missingness and modality imbalance. Missingness-resilient backbones such as ShaSpec (Wang et al., 2023), Flex-MoE (Yun et al., 2024), and MAESTRO (Mohapatra et al., 2025) support prediction with incomplete inputs, providing trained mechanisms for modality omission. Meanwhile, joint training can favor dominant modalities and under-optimize others (Wang et al., 2020b; Peng et al., 2022). OGM (Peng et al., 2022) modulates gradients according to relative modality contributions, while OPM (Wei et al., 2024) dynamically drops modality features based on discriminative-performance discrepancies. MIAM (Zbinden et al., 2026) adjusts token-masking distributions using modality-specific performance and learning dynamics, supporting incomplete inputs within and across modalities. OPM and MIAM thus already connect adaptive masking with missing-input robustness. CARAT builds on these capabilities to address a further decision: whether a currently abnormal stream can be discarded. Its windowed gradient norms guide missingness exposure and produce a frozen, backbone-specific reliance proxy, which combines with deployment-time abnormality flags to select joint removal or pre-fusion attenuation of the suspect set. 

Time-series shift detection and adaptation. Time-series methods detect anomalies through reconstruction-based scoring (Xu et al., 2023; Sun et al.) or association discrepancy (Xu et al., 2021), and adapt through spectral alignment and temporal normalization (Gnassounou et al., 2024; 2025). Forecasting methods address nonstationarity through test-time calibration or shift-aware updates (Kim et al., 2025; Grover & Etemad, 2025). CARAT combines reconstruction error and power spectral density distance relative to clean source statistics to flag suspect streams, complementing its backbone-dependence proxy with observation-level abnormality information. Detector outputs enter routing as per-modality flags, permitting alternative detectors; Section 4.2 examines sensitivity to imperfect flags.

Our objective is to control modality participation and update a small subset of model parameters using only unlabeled test batches {B t}, without access to raw source samples during adaptation, to improve robustness under multimodal corruption. 3.2 OUR APPROACH 

We propose CARAT (Conditional modality composition and reliance-guided at tenuation for efficient test-time adaptation), a multimodal test-time adaptation framework that combines current input abnormality with source-learned modality reliance. A missingness-resilient backbone supplies a trained operation for omitting inputs; the reliance profile helps decide when to use it. CARAT routes each sample between removal and attenuation without evaluating candidate modality subsets. During training, we replace uniform modality dropout with an asymmetric curriculum that modulates each modality’s dropout rate using its windowed input-projection gradient norm, yielding a reliance profile 

{ρj }Mj=1 at convergence (Section 3.2.1). At test time, a lightweight per-modality one-class detector identifies suspect inputs (Section 3.2.2), and the learnt reliance prior determines the intervention. If the unflagged modalities retain sufficient reliance mass, all suspect inputs to Ej are replaced with the missingness symbol; otherwise, the suspect modalities are propagated but attenuated prior to fusion in F . Adaptation proceeds by updating only normalization and attenuation parameters using a sample-weighted entropy objective over unlabeled test batches. For each test sample Mi, a per-modality detector Dj (·) flags suspect modalities. The detector assesses current input abnormality; the source-derived profile guides the intervention. The frozen reliance profile {ρj } then determines the intervention: if the surviving (unflagged) modalities account for a sufficient share of the reliance budget, the suspect inputs are replaced with the missingness symbol s0 at the input to Ej (dropped); otherwise, they propagate but their pre-fusion contribution is dampened by a learnable attenuator before F .

Suspect modality detection. For each sample Mi and modality j, a lightweight one-class detector 

Dj (·) computes a corruption score using reconstruction and spectral-distance features relative to compact clean-reference statistics computed during source training (Xu et al., 2023; Gnassounou et al., 2024; 2025). No raw source data is accessed during adaptation. Modalities whose score exceeds a fixed threshold are flagged as suspect, producing the routing set Si. Since CARAT consumes only this binary routing signal, the framework remains agnostic to the specific detector choice. The detectors are trained offline and reused at deployment. Appendix D details their formulation and sensitivity analyses, while Appendix L.1 reports their training cost, detector-only inference latency, memory footprint, and stored artifacts.

## 4 RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

We evaluate whether source-learned reliance helps a missingness-resilient backbone decide when to remove suspect inputs and when to retain them through attenuation. The experiments test overall robustness and deployment cost (Section 4.1), then examine the contributions of source preparation, reliance ordering, detection, and online updates (Section 4.2). 

Benchmark. We use four wearable sensing datasets: DSADS (Altun et al., 2010) (19-class activity recognition from five body-worn IMUs), PAMAP2 (Reiss & Stricker, 2012; de Frana, n.d.) (12-class activity recognition from three IMUs and heart rate), DaliaHAR (Reiss et al., 2019) (7-class activity recognition from wrist motion and physiological signals plus chest acceleration), and WE-SAD (Schmidt et al., 2018) (3-class stress classification from ten chest and wrist streams). The benchmark spans IMU-dominated and heterogeneous sensing configurations. We apply five synthetic sensor corruptions to selected modality subsets: discrete missingness, waveform anomalies, nonlinear degradation, frequency-selective attenuation, and nonstationary noise. The complete grids contain 50, 50, 45, and 95 conditions per backbone on DSADS, DaliaHAR, PAMAP2, and WESAD, respectively. Appendix B details the sensors and splits; Appendix A specifies corruption models and parameters, with examples in Figure 7. 

Backbones and comparators. We evaluate MAESTRO (Mohapatra et al., 2025), ShaSpec (Wang et al., 2023), and Flex-MoE (Yun et al., 2024), which differ in their encoders and fusion mechanisms but support missing inputs and modality-specific attenuation before fusion. Comparators comprise Static (no adaptation) and eight TTA methods: TENT (Wang et al., 2020a), EATA (Niu et al., 2022), and SoTTA (Gong et al., 2023); multimodal methods READ (Yang et al., 2024), SuMi (Guo & Jin, 2025), AeO (Dong et al., 2025), and PTA (Wang et al.); and time-series-specific PSDNorm (Gnas-sounou et al., 2025). In the main benchmark, all methods share the same source checkpoint trained with our asymmetric curriculum and the same corrupted test batches. Table 1 yields three key observations. (i) CARAT achieves the highest overall macro-F1 and best mean rank. It improves on EATA, the strongest baseline overall, by 1.58 F1 points on average, with a median paired improvement of 0.75 points across the 12 settings. Its mean rank is 2.42, compared with 3.50 for EATA. (ii) The largest gains occur on ShaSpec across all four datasets. 

CARAT leads in every ShaSpec setting, averaging 4.39 points above EATA, compared with 0.15 on MAESTRO and 0.19 on Flex-MoE. (iii) CARAT leads or remains close to the best method in 11 of the 12 settings. It ranks first in seven settings and third in four, with the latter trailing the best method by only 0.18–0.56 F1 points. Appendices H–K provide every modality-subset and corruption result. Appendix N reports paired effects, intervals, and condition-level ranks; its separate five-redraw DSADS–MAESTRO experiment reports positive paired intervals against all eight included comparators, assessing corruption variability for a fixed checkpoint. 

The benchmark reveals a structural trend: the relative strength of TTA methods changes with the sensing configuration. On IMU-dominated DSADS and PAMAP2, multimodal methods such as PTA are competitive; PTA is the strongest baseline on DSADS–MAESTRO and DSADS–Flex-MoE. On heterogeneous DaliaHAR and WESAD, however, TENT and EATA outperform PTA across all six dataset–backbone settings. With Flex-MoE, both obtain approximately 0.68 on DaliaHAR and 0.54 on WESAD, compared with PTA’s 0.60 and 0.50. A plausible explanation is that disagreement between sensors measuring different physical processes can reflect complementary information, making it an ambiguous cue for corruption. This observation motivates CARAT ’s separation of sensor abnormality from backbone reliance: current detector flags identify suspect inputs, while the source-learned prior guides their omission or attenuation. ## 5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

CARAT amortizes reliance estimation through source training, separating it from runtime corruption detection to guide omission or attenuation without subset search. Across 12 equally weighted dataset– backbone settings, it leads overall macro-F1 by 1.58 points over EATA and achieves the best mean rank (2.42), with the largest gains on ShaSpec. PTA is competitive on IMU-dominated datasets, whereas TENT and EATA are stronger on heterogeneous datasets. These findings support CARAT 

as a practical option in the evaluated setting. Evaluation covers synthetic wearable-classification corruptions and backbones supporting trained omission and separable pre-fusion representations. The frozen, parameterization-dependent prior may become stale, and attenuation cannot ensure reliability when all streams are uninformative. Future work targets slow-timescale prior refresh, uncertainty-aware adaptation and abstention, finer suspect-set selection, and validation under natural failures across broader tasks and architectures. 9Preprint

Connection to reliance-guided adaptation. The profile connects two stages of CARAT . During training, higher epoch-wise dropout probabilities increase exposure to inputs in which the correspond-ing modalities are absent. At deployment, the frozen terminal profile supplies the relative reliance mass used by the routing rule. Removal invokes the trained missingness operation, while attenuation retains downweighted suspect representations when the retained-reliance threshold is not met. These interventions act at different locations in the model; the construction does not establish equivalence of their representations or proximity to the source distribution. Their utility is assessed through the omission and prior-perturbation experiments in Section 4. 

Scope of the interpretation. The gradient-flow decomposition holds for the specified param-eterization and loss and requires no stationarity assumption. Finite-step optimization, adaptive preconditioning, stochastic batches, and changing dropout masks differ from this idealization. Pa-rameter scaling and projection width can also change the measured norms. We therefore use ρj as a backbone-specific routing prior, without interpreting it as a causal attribution of predictive information or a guarantee that a modality can be removed safely. The leave-one-modality-out comparison, prior perturbations, and width diagnostics provide empirical checks of this use.

## D DETECTOR ANALYSES 

Overview. Our suspect-modality detector is intentionally lightweight and serves only as a coarse routing signal for downstream adaptation rather than a standalone corruption classifier. For each sample Mi and modality j, the detector computes a corruption score sji from two complementary feature families: FITS reconstruction-error statistics and log-spectral-distance (LSD) features relative to clean source data. Modalities whose score exceeds a clean-reference threshold are flagged as suspect and routed accordingly during adaptation.

We evaluate the eight TTA baselines described in Section 4 under the same episodic protocol. In addition to these baselines, we report Static as the no-adaptation reference. For each test batch, the adaptation model is restored to the source checkpoint, adapted using only the current batch, and then used to predict the same batch. All baselines are evaluated on the same corrupted batches and modality-corruption scenarios. Unless otherwise specified, we follow the recommended hyperparameter settings of each baseline and use Adam with learning rate 10 −3 for test-time updates. ## G IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS OF DIFFERENT BACKBONES 

We evaluate CARAT on three missingness-resilient backbones: MAESTRO (Mohapatra et al., 2025), ShaSpec (Wang et al., 2023), and Flex-MoE (Yun et al., 2024). To keep the comparison across backbones fair, we preserve the original backbone architectures and supervised training objectives, while applying the asymmetric modality-dropout curriculum introduced in Section 3.2.1 during source training. This curriculum changes the modality masking schedule but does not modify the backbone loss or add CARAT -specific modules to the source model. It produces the backbone-specific reliance profile used by CARAT at test time. In our implementation, all source backbones are trained on SAX-tokenized source data. The checkpoint with the best validation accuracy is used as the source checkpoint for CARAT and all baseline TTA methods. 

MAESTRO. MAESTRO follows the original MAESTRO training objective and is optimized with cross-entropy loss over activity labels. During source training, we apply the asymmetric modality-dropout curriculum to estimate the MAESTRO-specific reliance profile. In the curriculum, the base modality-dropout probability is zero during the initial warm-up stage, increases linearly after warm-up, and is then held fixed. The per-modality dropout probabilities are assigned according to running gradient norms of the modality input projections, so modalities with larger estimated reliance are masked more often during training. 

Flex-MoE. Flex-MoE follows the original Flex-MoE supervised objective. The training loss combines cross-entropy classification loss with the gate load-balancing loss from its fusion module: 

L = Lce + λgate Lgate , λgate = 10 −2.

During source training, we apply the same asymmetric modality-dropout curriculum to estimate the Flex-MoE-specific reliance profile. The curriculum is applied to the modality inputs while keeping the Flex-MoE architecture and fusion objective unchanged. 

ShaSpec. ShaSpec follows the original ShaSpec shared-specific training objective, which combines task classification, shared-feature alignment, and modality-specific domain classification: 

L = Lce + αLshared + βLspecific , α = 0 .1, β = 0 .02 .

The shared loss aligns shared modality features, and the specific loss trains modality-domain predic-tions. During source training, we apply the same asymmetric modality-dropout curriculum to estimate the ShaSpec-specific reliance profile, while keeping the ShaSpec architecture and shared-specific objective unchanged. 21 Preprint G.1 MODALITY RELIANCE PROFILES ACROSS BACKBONES AND DATASETS

G.2 SENSITIVITY STUDY 

Figures 10 and 11 evaluate the sensitivity of CARAT to two adaptation hyperparameters using the same DaliaHAR + ShaSpec setup as the main ablations in Section 4.2. Across both the pooled diverse setting ( Div. ) and representative high-/low-reliance modalities ( w_acc , w_temp ), performance 22 Preprint remains stable across a broad range of episodic TTA batch sizes and routing thresholds. In particular, varying the routing threshold ν across {0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9} changes macro-F1 by less than 2% relative, suggesting that the routing mechanism depends more on the coarse separation between high- and low-reliance corruption regimes than on precise threshold tuning. We fix ν = 0 .8 across all datasets, backbones, and corruption types in the main results. More importantly, ν exposes a useful system-level knob to the deployer: smaller ν shifts more suspect modalities into the modality-composition path (drop and replace with s0), which avoids the per-modality attenuator forward pass and reduces test-time compute, at the cost of being more aggressive only toward genuinely low-reliance modalities; larger ν shifts more suspect modalities into the latent-attenuation path, preserving partially informative streams at modestly higher cost. This makes ν less of a hyperparameter to tune for accuracy and more of a deployment-time lever to trade efficiency against conservatism, with ν = 0 .8 providing a balanced default. 16 32 64 128

## L COMPLETE EFFICIENCY METRICS 

We report the complete efficiency measurements in Table 24 and Table 25. All measurements are conducted on corrupted test batches with batch size 64 . Latency is the average wall-clock time per batch after warm-up, including test-time adaptation and inference. For detector-based methods, the detector is executed as part of the method, so its overhead is included. Energy is measured as the average CPU energy per batch in Joules. GFLOPs are measured using the PyTorch profiler and averaged over test batches. Trainable parameters denote the number of parameters updated during test-time adaptation; non-parametric methods have zero trainable parameters. For DSADS, the efficiency benchmark corrupts the torso, right-arm, and left-arm modalities with

We separately widen one modality’s input projection eightfold and retrain DaliaHAR–MAESTRO with the remaining setup fixed: chest acceleration from hidden width 96 to 768, or wrist temperature from 32 to 256. Table 37 reports the normalized gradient-based reliance ratios rje⋆ , which precede the dropout transformation and should not be confused with the terminal dropout probabilities ρj in Table 36. The widened modality’s ratio increases by 2.90 percentage points for chest acceleration and 4.80 points for wrist temperature. Mean classification F1 changes from 80.30 to 80.20 and 79.50 points, respectively. These limited retraining controls do not show reliance increasing in proportion to the eightfold width change, but several other modalities’ ratios also change; they therefore do not establish parameterization invariance.
